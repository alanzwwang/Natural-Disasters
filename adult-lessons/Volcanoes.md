Introduction
─────────────────────────────────

Volcanism is the surface expression of the Earth's internal heat engine: the transfer of mass and energy from the mantle and crust to the surface, the oceans and the atmosphere. About 1,300–1,400 volcanoes are known to have erupted during the Holocene (the last ~11,700 years), according to the Smithsonian Institution's Global Volcanism Program; larger counts of roughly 1,500 "active" volcanoes include centres whose recent activity is less certain, and the great majority of submarine volcanoes on the mid-ocean ridges are not counted at all. In a typical year some 50–70 volcanoes erupt, and on any given day roughly 20–45 eruptions are in progress.

Volcanic hazards are unusual among natural hazards in three respects. First, they are extraordinarily diverse: a single eruption can produce lava flows, pyroclastic density currents, tephra fall, ballistic blocks, lahars, debris avalanches, tsunamis, toxic gases and, at the largest scales, global climate perturbation. Second, they span an enormous range of magnitude — erupted volumes range over about nine orders of magnitude, from small lava fountains of a few thousand cubic metres to "super-eruptions" exceeding 1,000 km³. Third, unlike earthquakes, most eruptions are preceded by measurable unrest: magma must physically move towards the surface, and in doing so it fractures rock, deforms the ground and releases gas. Volcanology is therefore one of the few hazard sciences in which short-term forecasting is routinely possible, although it remains probabilistic and frequently ambiguous.

The historical record compiled by Auker et al. (2013) lists roughly 280,000 volcanic fatalities between 1600 and 2010, dominated by a handful of catastrophes (Tambora 1815, Krakatau 1883, Mont Pelée 1902, Nevado del Ruiz 1985, Laki 1783). Around 800 million people are estimated to live within 100 km of a Holocene volcano, concentrated in Indonesia, the Philippines, Japan, Central America, the Andes, East Africa and Italy. Aviation, global supply chains and the climate system extend volcanic risk far beyond the immediate surroundings of a volcano.

This course treats volcanoes at a graduate level, following the chain from the generation of magma in the mantle, through its ascent, degassing and fragmentation, to the physics of eruption columns and hazardous flows, and finally to monitoring, forecasting, climate impacts, aviation and risk reduction. Each lesson pairs the governing physics with order-of-magnitude reasoning and worked exercises, and the course closes with eight case studies that illustrate how the science was learned — often at great cost.

In this course, you will:

  1. Explain how decompression, flux and heat-transfer melting generate magma in different plate-tectonic settings, and estimate melting depths from simple solidus–adiabat models
  2. Relate magma viscosity to composition, temperature, dissolved water and crystal content, and use simple flow laws to estimate lava velocities
  3. Quantify volatile solubility, exsolution and vesiculation, and explain the main criteria for magma fragmentation
  4. Classify eruption styles, and compute eruption magnitude, intensity and Volcanic Explosivity Index from erupted mass and duration
  5. Apply buoyant-plume theory and empirical scaling to eruption columns, and explain the conditions for column collapse
  6. Describe the physics and hazards of pyroclastic density currents, lahars, lava flows, tephra fall and volcanic gases
  7. Interpret seismic, geodetic and gas monitoring data, and use simple source and forecasting models
  8. Explain how volcanic sulfur alters stratospheric aerosol and climate, and estimate radiative forcing from aerosol optical depth
  9. Evaluate the hazard of volcanic ash to aviation and the role of the Volcanic Ash Advisory Centres
  10. Construct hazard maps and risk estimates, and evaluate lessons from historical eruptions for risk reduction

Lesson 1: Plate Settings and Magma Genesis
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        The mantle is almost entirely solid; magma forms only where rock crosses its solidus — by decompression, by adding volatiles (flux), or by adding heat

Where volcanoes occur

Roughly three-quarters of the Earth's annual magma production occurs at divergent plate boundaries, where the mid-ocean ridge system generates about 20 km³ of new oceanic crust each year, almost entirely unseen beneath the sea. Subduction zones host most of the subaerial, explosive and dangerous volcanoes — the Pacific "Ring of Fire", the Indonesian (Sunda–Banda) arc, the Lesser Antilles, the Aegean and the Aleutians. Intraplate "hotspot" volcanism (Hawaiʻi, Réunion, Galápagos, Yellowstone) and continental rifts (East Africa, Iceland where a hotspot coincides with a ridge) make up the remainder. Estimates of the global volumetric output of magma are of order 20–35 km³ per year, of which only a small fraction — perhaps one-fifth to one-tenth — reaches the surface; the rest crystallises as intrusions. These figures are uncertain by tens of per cent.

Decompression melting

The upper mantle is peridotite, dominated by olivine with orthopyroxene, clinopyroxene and an aluminous phase. Its dry solidus at the surface is near 1,120 °C and rises with pressure by roughly 130 °C per GPa at low pressures; a commonly used fit (Hirschmann, 2000) is approximately:

        T_{solidus} (°C) ≈ 1120 + 132.9 P − 5.1 P²      (P in GPa)

Convecting mantle follows an adiabat whose temperature changes only slowly with depth — of order 0.3–0.5 °C per km for solid mantle — anchored by the mantle potential temperature T_{p}, the temperature the mantle would have if brought adiabatically to the surface without melting, generally estimated at 1,300–1,400 °C beneath ridges and 100–300 °C hotter beneath major hotspots. Because the solidus slope is much steeper than the adiabat, mantle rising beneath a ridge or plume crosses its solidus at some depth and begins to melt. Once melting begins, latent heat is consumed and the temperature follows a shallower path; melt productivity is of order 10–20% per GPa of further decompression (roughly 0.3–0.6% per km). Beneath normal ridges melting begins at 60–80 km and the average melt fraction of the melting column is about 10%, producing an oceanic crust about 6–7 km thick (McKenzie and Bickle, 1988, provided the classic analysis). Hotter plume mantle starts melting deeper, melts more, and builds thicker crust, as in Iceland, where crust reaches 20–40 km.

Flux melting at subduction zones

Subducting oceanic lithosphere carries water in altered crust, sediments and serpentinised mantle. As the slab heats and pressure rises, hydrous minerals (amphibole, lawsonite, chlorite, serpentine) break down and release fluids into the overlying mantle wedge. Water dramatically depresses the peridotite solidus — by several hundred degrees at water saturation — so that the hot core of the wedge melts even without decompression. Arc volcanoes typically sit above the point where the slab reaches about 70–170 km depth, most commonly around 100 km, a remarkably consistent geometry that reflects the combination of slab dehydration and the thermal structure of the wedge. Primary arc magmas are hydrous basalts carrying perhaps 2–6 wt% H₂O, which is why arc volcanoes are far more explosive than ridge or hotspot volcanoes.

Heat-transfer melting and crustal processing

When mantle-derived basalt stalls in the crust, it cools and crystallises, releasing sensible heat and latent heat of crystallisation. That heat can partially melt the surrounding crust, which has a much lower solidus (roughly 650–750 °C for water-bearing granitic compositions). This "heat-transfer" or crustal anatexis process, combined with fractional crystallisation and assimilation (the AFC model), produces the evolved, silica-rich andesites, dacites and rhyolites characteristic of continental arcs and large silicic calderas. Modern work views most crustal magma reservoirs as "trans-crustal mush systems": vertically extensive regions of crystal-rich mush with transient, melt-rich lenses, rather than the classic single, liquid-filled magma chamber.

Why magma rises

Magma rises because it is less dense than its surroundings. Basaltic melt (~2,700–2,800 kg m⁻³ at depth) is lighter than mantle peridotite (~3,300 kg m⁻³), and the buoyancy pressure gradient Δρ g drives porous flow through the partially molten mantle and, at higher melt fractions, fracture propagation as dykes. In the crust, magma may reach a level of neutral buoyancy where its density equals that of the host rock, which is one reason magma reservoirs form at characteristic depths of a few kilometres to ~15 km.

Lesson 2: Magma Rheology and Petrology
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Viscosity spans more than eleven orders of magnitude, from ~10 Pa s for hot basalt to >10¹² Pa s for degassed rhyolite, and it controls whether gas escapes quietly or explosively

Composition and classification

Volcanic rocks are classified chemically by silica and total alkalis (the TAS diagram). In approximate terms, basalt contains 45–52 wt% SiO₂, basaltic andesite 52–57%, andesite 57–63%, dacite 63–~69%, and rhyolite more than ~69%. Eruption temperatures fall as silica rises: basalts erupt at roughly 1,100–1,250 °C, andesites at 950–1,100 °C, dacites at 800–1,000 °C and rhyolites at 700–900 °C. Silica-rich melts are also typically richer in dissolved water, because water is concentrated in the residual liquid as anhydrous crystals form.

Melt structure and viscosity

Silicate melts are networks of SiO₄ tetrahedra linked by bridging oxygens. Network-forming cations (Si⁴⁺, Al³⁺ when charge-balanced) increase polymerisation and viscosity; network modifiers (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺, Na⁺, K⁺) and dissolved water break bridging bonds and lower viscosity. Over the range of eruption temperatures, melt viscosity follows a non-Arrhenian Vogel–Fulcher–Tammann (VFT) form:

        log₁₀ η = A + B / (T − C)

where T is in kelvin and A, B, C depend on composition. The model of Giordano, Russell and Dingwell (2008) fits a common A ≈ −4.55 and composition-dependent B and C, and is widely used. Typical melt viscosities are:

        Composition       T (°C)        η (Pa s)
        Basalt            1,150–1,250   10¹–10³
        Andesite          1,000–1,100   10³–10⁶
        Dacite            900–1,000     10⁵–10⁸
        Hydrous rhyolite  750–850       10⁶–10⁹
        Degassed rhyolite 750–850       10¹⁰–10¹²

Adding 1 wt% of water to a dry rhyolite can lower its viscosity by two to three orders of magnitude; conversely, degassing during ascent raises viscosity enormously, which is one reason why ascending silicic magma can "freeze" in the conduit and why lava domes are so stiff.

Crystals and bubbles

Natural magmas are suspensions. Crystals raise the effective viscosity, modestly at low concentrations and catastrophically as the crystal fraction φ approaches the maximum packing fraction φ_{m} (typically ~0.5–0.7, depending on crystal shape and size distribution). A simple relation is the Einstein–Roscoe equation:

        η_{eff} = η_{melt} (1 − φ/φ_{m})^{−2.5}

Above roughly 40–50% crystals a magma develops a yield strength and behaves as a Bingham or Herschel–Bulkley fluid; above ~60% it behaves essentially as a solid "mush" that cannot erupt without remobilisation (for example by reheating or gas sparging from fresh basalt injected from below). Bubbles can either increase or decrease viscosity depending on the capillary number, i.e. on whether they deform in the flow.

Lava flow dynamics

A lava flow on a slope is driven by gravity and resisted by viscous stresses. For a wide sheet of Newtonian fluid of thickness h, density ρ and viscosity η on a slope θ, the Jeffreys equation gives the mean velocity:

        ū = ρ g h² sin θ / (3 η)

Basaltic flows advance at metres per second near the vent (and up to tens of km/h in confined channels), whereas andesite and dacite lavas move metres per day and dacitic domes grow by extrusion of near-solid magma. As lava cools, a crust develops and the flow becomes rheologically complex: basaltic flows form smooth, ropy pāhoehoe at low strain rates and clinkery ʻaʻā at higher strain rates and viscosities. Flow length is limited by effusion rate and cooling, which is why high effusion rates produce long flows (Walker's classical observation).

Lesson 3: Volatiles, Vesiculation and Fragmentation
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Dissolved water and CO₂ come out of solution as pressure falls; when the expanding gas fraction or strain rate becomes large enough, the melt breaks into fragments and an explosive eruption begins

Volatile species and solubility

The principal magmatic volatiles are H₂O (typically 0.1–0.5 wt% in ridge basalts, 2–6 wt% in arc magmas, and up to ~6–7 wt% in some rhyolites), CO₂, sulfur species (SO₂, H₂S), and halogens (HCl, HF). Water solubility in silicate melts increases approximately with the square root of pressure, reflecting dissolution of water partly as hydroxyl groups. For rhyolite a useful approximation is:

        c = s P^{1/2},    s ≈ 4.1 × 10⁻⁶ Pa^{−1/2}

where c is the mass fraction of dissolved water. This gives about 4 wt% at 100 MPa (~4 km depth) and about 6 wt% at 200 MPa. CO₂ is far less soluble — of order a thousand parts per million at hundreds of MPa — so it exsolves at much greater depth, and the deep CO₂ flux can provide early warning of fresh magma (as at Kīlauea and Etna). Sulfur solubility depends strongly on oxidation state and on the presence of sulfide or anhydrite.

Nucleation and bubble growth

When magma rises above its saturation pressure, it becomes supersaturated and bubbles nucleate — homogeneously at high supersaturation (of order 100 MPa or more in crystal-poor rhyolite) or heterogeneously on crystals such as Fe–Ti oxides at much lower supersaturation. Bubbles then grow by diffusion of volatiles from the melt and by decompression expansion. In low-viscosity basalt, bubbles can rise relative to the melt, coalesce, and separate from it, producing Strombolian bursts of large gas slugs; in viscous silicic magma, bubbles remain trapped and grow together with the melt (closed-system degassing) until the gas fraction becomes very large.

The gas volume fraction

For a mass fraction n of exsolved water at pressure P and temperature T, treated as an ideal gas with molar mass M = 0.018 kg mol⁻¹, the volume of gas per kilogram of magma is n R T / (M P), and the gas volume fraction is:

        α = (n R T / M P) / (n R T / M P + 1/ρ_{melt})

Because P falls by orders of magnitude in the upper few kilometres, α rises rapidly; even a few weight per cent of water produces gas volume fractions above 99% at atmospheric pressure. This is the root of explosive volcanism.

Fragmentation criteria

Fragmentation is the transition from a continuous melt containing bubbles to a continuous gas carrying fragments of melt (pyroclasts). Several criteria have been proposed and are probably all relevant in different settings. The classical volume-fraction criterion (Sparks, 1978) places fragmentation where α reaches ~0.75–0.8, near the limit of bubble packing; many pumices indeed have vesicularities of 70–85%. The strain-rate criterion (Papale, 1999) states that magma fragments when the elongational strain rate exceeds the inverse of its structural relaxation time, i.e. when the melt responds as a brittle solid (a glass transition crossed by deformation rate rather than temperature). The overpressure criterion holds that bubble walls fail when the gas overpressure exceeds the tensile strength of the melt (of order a few MPa), which shock-tube experiments confirm. Permeability plays a crucial competing role: if bubbles connect and gas escapes through the magma or conduit walls (open-system degassing), the magma can erupt effusively as lava domes even when initially volatile-rich. The balance between ascent rate and outgassing is therefore a central control on whether a silicic eruption is explosive or effusive.

Magma–water interaction

External water adds a second explosive pathway. Phreatomagmatic eruptions result from rapid heat transfer from magma to groundwater, lake or sea water, generating steam explosions and very fine ash; molten fuel–coolant interaction can achieve conversion efficiencies of thermal to mechanical energy of several per cent or more. Phreatic (steam-driven) eruptions involve no fresh magma at the surface and can occur with very little warning, as at Ontake (Japan) in 2014, which killed 63 hikers, and Whakaari/White Island (New Zealand) in 2019, which killed 22.

Lesson 4: Eruption Styles and the Volcanic Explosivity Index
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Eruptions are characterised by magnitude (total mass erupted) and intensity (mass eruption rate); the VEI blends both into a single logarithmic index

A spectrum of eruption styles

Eruption styles are named after type volcanoes and form a continuum rather than discrete classes. Hawaiian eruptions produce lava fountains (tens to hundreds of metres, exceptionally >1 km) and lava flows from low-viscosity basalt. Strombolian activity consists of discrete bursts every few seconds to minutes as gas slugs reach the surface, throwing incandescent bombs tens to a few hundred metres. Vulcanian eruptions are short, violent cannon-like explosions that clear a plugged vent, producing ballistic blocks and columns several to ~20 km high. Plinian eruptions — named after Pliny the Younger's account of Vesuvius in AD 79 — are sustained, high-intensity discharges of fragmented silicic magma lasting hours to days, with columns of 20–45 km and widespread pumice fall; sub-Plinian eruptions are their smaller, more unsteady relatives. Peléan activity, named after Mont Pelée in 1902, involves the growth and collapse of lava domes, generating block-and-ash flows. Surtseyan and other phreatomagmatic eruptions involve explosive interaction with water. At the largest scale, caldera-forming eruptions evacuate reservoirs so rapidly that the roof collapses, generating ignimbrites (deposits of large pyroclastic density currents) covering thousands of square kilometres.

The Walker classification

George Walker (1973) proposed classifying fall deposits by their dispersal (the area enclosed by the isopach equal to 1% of the maximum thickness) and fragmentation (the proportion of fine particles). Hawaiian and Strombolian deposits are poorly dispersed and coarse; Plinian deposits are widely dispersed; phreatoplinian deposits are both widely dispersed and very fine. This quantitative approach to deposits remains the basis of physical volcanology, because most eruptions in the geological record are known only from their deposits.

Magnitude, intensity and VEI

Following Pyle (2000), magnitude and intensity are defined from erupted mass:

        M = log₁₀(erupted mass, kg) − 7
        I = log₁₀(mass eruption rate, kg s⁻¹) + 3

The Volcanic Explosivity Index (Newhall and Self, 1982) is a 0–8 scale based mainly on bulk tephra volume, supplemented by column height and qualitative descriptors. Each step above VEI 2 represents a factor of ten in volume:

        VEI   Bulk tephra volume     Column height   Example
        0     < 10⁴ m³               < 100 m         Kīlauea lava lakes
        1     10⁴–10⁶ m³             100 m–1 km      Stromboli (typical)
        2     10⁶–10⁷ m³             1–5 km          Galeras 1993
        3     10⁷–10⁸ m³             3–15 km         Nevado del Ruiz 1985
        4     0.1–1 km³              10–25 km        Eyjafjallajökull 2010
        5     1–10 km³               > 25 km         Mount St. Helens 1980
        6     10–100 km³             > 25 km         Pinatubo 1991, Krakatau 1883
        7     100–1,000 km³          > 25 km         Tambora 1815
        8     > 1,000 km³            > 25 km         Toba ~74 ka, Yellowstone 2.08 Ma

Frequency–magnitude relations show that small eruptions are vastly more common than large ones. Approximate global return periods are: VEI 4 a few times per decade, VEI 5 roughly once every one to two decades, VEI 6 one to two per century, VEI 7 one to two per millennium, and VEI 8 perhaps once every few tens of thousands of years — though the record of large eruptions is incomplete even for the last few centuries, and estimates for the largest eruptions carry wide uncertainty (one analysis of the geological record, Rougier et al. 2018, suggested a mean interval for magnitude-8 eruptions of ~17,000 years with a range of roughly 5,000–48,000 years).

Limitations of VEI

The VEI is useful for cataloguing but has important shortcomings: it is insensitive to effusive eruptions (the Laki eruption of 1783, which released ~15 km³ of lava and had enormous atmospheric impact, rates only VEI 4); it ignores sulfur yield, which controls climatic impact; and volumetric thresholds depend on bulk deposit density, which varies widely. Volumes are therefore often given as dense-rock equivalent (DRE), the volume the magma would occupy without vesicles, typically one-half to one-third of the bulk tephra volume.

Lesson 5: Eruption Columns and Plume Dynamics
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        A Plinian column rises because it entrains and heats air until it becomes less dense than the atmosphere; if it cannot entrain enough air quickly enough, it collapses into pyroclastic density currents

Structure of an eruption column

A sustained eruption column has three regions. The gas-thrust region, a few hundred metres to a few kilometres high, is a momentum-driven jet: the erupting mixture leaves the vent at speeds of order 100–600 m s⁻¹, but because it is largely solid and liquid particles by mass, it is initially several times denser than the atmosphere. The jet entrains ambient air through turbulent eddies at its margins; the air is heated by the hot pyroclasts and expands, so the mixture density drops. If the density falls below that of the atmosphere before the jet's momentum is exhausted, the column enters the convective region and rises buoyantly as a plume. It eventually reaches a level of neutral buoyancy in the stably stratified atmosphere, overshoots by momentum, and spreads laterally as an umbrella cloud — a gravity current that can extend hundreds of kilometres even upwind.

Buoyant plume theory

Morton, Taylor and Turner (1956) modelled turbulent plumes by assuming that the inflow velocity of entrained air is proportional to the plume's local vertical velocity, with an entrainment coefficient of about 0.1. For a plume from a maintained point source of buoyancy flux F₀ (units m⁴ s⁻³) rising through an atmosphere of constant buoyancy frequency N, dimensional analysis and the MTT equations give:

        H = C F₀^{1/4} N^{−3/4}

with C of order 5–8 depending on the entrainment coefficient and whether the maximum or neutral-buoyancy height is meant. Because the buoyancy flux is proportional to the thermal energy flux, and thus to the mass eruption rate, column height scales with the fourth root of eruption rate: a tenfold increase in eruption rate raises the column by only a factor of ~1.8. For practical use, Mastin et al. (2009) fitted historical eruptions to the empirical relation:

        H = 2.00 V̇^{0.241}

where H is the plume height above the vent in km and V̇ the DRE volumetric eruption rate in m³ s⁻¹. The scatter is large — roughly a factor of four in eruption rate for a given height — and wind strongly bends weak plumes, reducing their rise height; models such as those of Woods (1988) and Bursik (2001) include moisture, wind and particle fallout. Latent heat released by condensation of entrained water vapour can add several kilometres to the height of a column in a humid tropical atmosphere.

Column collapse

If the mixture at the top of the gas-thrust region remains denser than the atmosphere, the column collapses and the mass falls back as a fountain feeding pyroclastic density currents. Wilson, Sparks and Walker (1980) showed that collapse is favoured by low exit velocity (low gas content), large vent radius (less air entrained relative to mass flux, since entrainment scales with surface area while mass flux scales with cross-sectional area), and low magma temperature. As a Plinian eruption progresses, vent erosion widens the conduit and the eruption may evolve from a stable column to partial and then total collapse — the classical sequence of Plinian fall overlain by ignimbrite seen at Vesuvius in AD 79 and in many caldera-forming eruptions. Partial collapse, with simultaneous buoyant plume and currents, is common and is now explored with three-dimensional multiphase numerical models.

Overpressured vents and supersonic jets

Eruption velocities are limited by the speed of sound in the gas–particle mixture, which can be only tens to ~100 m s⁻¹ because the particles add mass but not stiffness. At the vent, flow is often choked; if exit pressure exceeds atmospheric, the jet expands supersonically, forming shock structures (Mach discs) that have been imaged in laboratory and numerical experiments and inferred from infrasound. The 2022 Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai plume reached about 57 km, into the mesosphere, the highest volcanic plume ever measured, illustrating the extreme energetics possible when vast amounts of magma and seawater interact.

Tephra sedimentation

Particles fall out of the column margins and from the umbrella cloud. Coarse lapilli fall close to the vent; fine ash is carried hundreds to thousands of kilometres. Very fine ash (< 30–60 μm) commonly falls as aggregates, which brings it down faster and nearer to the vent than its individual settling velocity implies, producing secondary thickness maxima such as the one observed ~300 km downwind of Mount St. Helens in 1980.

Lesson 6: Pyroclastic Density Currents, Lahars, Lava, Tephra and Gas
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Pyroclastic density currents and lahars cause most volcanic deaths; tephra and gas cause the most widespread disruption

Pyroclastic density currents

Pyroclastic density currents (PDCs) are hot, gravity-driven mixtures of gas and volcanic particles that flow along the ground. They form by column collapse, by gravitational or explosive collapse of lava domes (block-and-ash flows), and by lateral blasts. They are conceptually stratified: a dense, concentrated basal underflow (granular flow, high particle concentration) channelled by topography, overlain by a dilute, turbulent, ash-cloud surge that can detach and cross ridges and water. Speeds range from ~10 m s⁻¹ to over 100 m s⁻¹ and temperatures typically from 200 °C to 700 °C. The dynamic pressure of a current, p_{dyn} = ½ ρ u², combined with its heat, is lethal: a few kilopascals breaks windows and doors and injures people, and tens of kilopascals destroy buildings. Even brief exposure to gas above ~200 °C causes fatal airway burns. Survivors are rare and are usually sheltered at the dilute margins. At Saint-Pierre (Martinique) on 8 May 1902, a current from Mont Pelée killed about 28,000 people within minutes; at Herculaneum in AD 79, surges overwhelmed the people sheltering by the shore. The mobility of large currents is often expressed by the ratio of fall height to runout length (H/L, the "Heim coefficient"), which decreases with volume, from ~0.3 for small block-and-ash flows to below 0.1 for large ignimbrites, which can run out more than 100 km and climb over topographic barriers more than 1 km high.

Lahars and debris avalanches

Lahars are volcanic mudflows and debris flows — water-saturated mixtures with sediment concentrations high enough (typically more than ~60% sediment by volume) to behave as non-Newtonian fluids with yield strength. They form when eruptions melt snow and ice, when crater lakes are breached, when heavy rain remobilises fresh tephra (as for years after Pinatubo), or when debris avalanches transform downstream. They travel at up to several tens of km/h (peak speeds of ~10–20 m s⁻¹ in steep, confined channels), follow river valleys, and can bulk up by eroding their beds, growing to several times their initial volume. Lahars killed about 23,000 people at Armero, Colombia, in 1985 — the deadliest volcanic event since 1902. Debris avalanches are giant landslides of the volcanic edifice itself; the 1980 Mount St. Helens collapse (~2.5 km³) is the largest in recorded history, and prehistoric collapses of ocean-island volcanoes reach hundreds of cubic kilometres.

Lava flows and ballistics

Lava flows rarely kill people — they usually advance slowly enough to escape — but they destroy everything in their path. Exceptions include the extremely fluid nephelinitic lavas of Nyiragongo (DR Congo), which in 1977 travelled at up to ~60 km/h and in 2002 flowed into the city of Goma. Diversion by barriers and water cooling has had mixed success (Heimaey, Iceland, 1973; Etna 1983 and 1992). Ballistic projectiles, ejected on near-parabolic trajectories, are hazardous within a few kilometres of the vent; drag reduces range for small blocks, but blocks larger than ~0.5 m follow trajectories close to the drag-free parabola.

Tephra fall

Tephra fall is the most widespread direct hazard. The thickness of many fall deposits decreases approximately exponentially with the square root of the area enclosed by an isopach (Pyle, 1989):

        T(A) = T₀ exp(−k √A),     V = 2 T₀ / k²

where T₀ is the extrapolated maximum thickness and V the deposit volume. Hazards include roof collapse under loads of a few kilopascals (dry ash 10 cm thick loads ~0.7–1.0 kPa; wet ash can be twice as heavy), contamination of water supplies, damage to crops and livestock, short-circuiting of electrical insulators, disruption of transport, and respiratory irritation; crystalline silica (cristobalite) in some ashes is a chronic health concern. At Pinatubo in 1991, most of the roughly 300–350 direct deaths resulted from roofs collapsing under rain-soaked ash during Typhoon Yunya.

Volcanic gases

Gases pose hazards at three scales. Locally, CO₂ — denser than air — accumulates in depressions; the 1986 limnic eruption of Lake Nyos (Cameroon), in which CO₂ accumulated in the lake from magmatic sources was suddenly released, suffocated about 1,700–1,750 people and some 3,500 livestock. Controlled degassing pipes have since been installed in Lakes Nyos and Monoun. Regionally, SO₂ forms sulfate aerosol ("vog"), and HF can poison grazing animals by fluorosis, as during Laki in 1783. Globally, stratospheric sulfate aerosol perturbs climate (Lesson 8).

Lesson 7: Monitoring and Forecasting
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Magma on the move breaks rock, deforms the ground and releases gas; combining seismic, geodetic and geochemical observations allows probabilistic forecasts of eruptions

Volcano seismology

Seismic monitoring is the backbone of volcano surveillance. Volcano-tectonic (VT) earthquakes are ordinary shear failures (dominant frequencies above ~5 Hz) caused by stress changes around intruding magma. Long-period (LP) events (~0.5–5 Hz) are attributed to resonance of fluid-filled cracks and conduits or to slow failure in weak, shallow material; swarms of LP events preceded the 1985 Nevado del Ruiz eruption and the 1989–1990 Redoubt eruptions, the latter providing a famous successful short-term forecast by Bernard Chouet and colleagues. Very-long-period (VLP) signals (periods of ~2–100 s) reflect mass movements of magma and gas. Volcanic tremor is a continuous signal lasting minutes to months, linked to sustained flow of fluids. Real-time Seismic Amplitude Measurement (RSAM) and Seismic Spectral Amplitude Measurement (SSAM), developed by the USGS, condense seismicity into time series that observatories track continuously. Infrasound arrays detect explosions, including at remote volcanoes, and global networks detected the Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai pressure wave around the world.

Ground deformation

Pressurisation of a magma reservoir inflates the ground surface. The simplest model, attributed to Mogi (1958), treats the reservoir as a point pressure source at depth d in an elastic half-space with Poisson's ratio ν. The vertical surface displacement at radial distance r for a source volume change ΔV is:

        u_{z}(r) = (1 − ν) ΔV d / [π (d² + r²)^{3/2}]

so that the maximum uplift above the source is (1 − ν) ΔV / (π d²), and the ratio of horizontal to vertical displacement is r/d. More realistic models include finite spheres, prolate and oblate ellipsoids, dykes and sills (Okada dislocations), topography, crustal heterogeneity and viscoelasticity; the volume change of the reservoir is not equal to the volume of injected magma because magma is compressible.

Deformation is measured by levelling and electronic distance measurement (historically), by borehole and platform tiltmeters (sensitive to sub-microradian tilts), by continuous GNSS (millimetre precision in all three components), and by Interferometric Synthetic Aperture Radar (InSAR). InSAR measures the change in range between two radar acquisitions along the satellite's line of sight; each interferometric fringe corresponds to half a radar wavelength (about 2.8 cm for C-band Sentinel-1 with λ ≈ 5.55 cm). Since the 1990s InSAR has revealed inflation at volcanoes thought to be dormant (e.g. Uturuncu, Bolivia; Laguna del Maule, Chile) and has documented the deflation that accompanies caldera collapse, as at Kīlauea in 2018 and Bárðarbunga in 2014–2015. Not all inflation leads to eruption; only a minority of deforming volcanoes erupt within years, and caldera "unrest" can persist for decades without eruption (Campi Flegrei, Italy; Long Valley, California; Yellowstone).

Gas geochemistry

Gas emissions provide a direct window on degassing magma. SO₂ is measured remotely by ultraviolet spectroscopy — correlation spectrometers (COSPEC) historically, and today by differential optical absorption spectroscopy (DOAS) instruments, scanning networks, UV cameras and satellites (TOMS, OMI, TROPOMI). Flux is obtained by integrating the column amount across the plume cross-section and multiplying by the plume speed. CO₂ and H₂S are measured in situ with Multi-GAS sensors; rising CO₂/SO₂ ratios can indicate deep, fresh magma, whereas falling SO₂ with rising seismicity may indicate sealing of the system and a build-up of pressure. Diffuse soil CO₂ degassing and thermal infrared imaging (from ground and satellite) complete the toolkit.

Forecasting

Forecasting combines observations with models of the eruption process. Voight (1988) proposed the Failure Forecast Method, based on the empirical relation for materials approaching failure:

        d²Ω/dt² = A (dΩ/dt)^{α}

where Ω is an observable such as cumulative seismic energy, strain or tilt. For α = 2, the inverse rate (dΩ/dt)⁻¹ decreases linearly with time and extrapolates to zero at the predicted failure time. The method has succeeded retrospectively and occasionally prospectively (e.g. dome explosions at Mount St. Helens in the 1980s), but often fails because of noise, changing processes, or failure of the system to reach eruption. In practice, observatories use expert elicitation and probabilistic event trees (e.g. BET_EF, Bayesian Event Tree for Eruption Forecasting), combining base rates from analogous volcanoes with monitoring data. Alerts are communicated by levels: the USGS ground-based system (Normal, Advisory, Watch, Warning) and the aviation colour code (Green, Yellow, Orange, Red), with many national systems in use worldwide. Notable forecasting successes include Pinatubo (1991), Merapi (2010, where some 350,000–400,000 people were evacuated and many thousands of lives were probably saved despite ~350–400 deaths), and the repeated evacuations at Soufrière Hills, Montserrat. Missed or ambiguous forecasts — Nevado del Ruiz (1985), Ontake (2014), Whakaari (2019) — highlight the difficulty of phreatic eruptions and of communicating uncertainty.

Lesson 8: Volcanic Aerosols and Climate
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Climatic impact depends on sulfur injected into the stratosphere, not on erupted volume; sulfate aerosols reflect sunlight and cool the surface for one to three years

From SO₂ to sulfate aerosol

Volcanic ash falls out of the atmosphere within days to weeks and has little effect on climate. Sulfur gases injected above the tropopause, however, are oxidised by the hydroxyl radical to sulfuric acid, which condenses with water into droplets of roughly 75% H₂SO₄ by mass. For the 1991 Pinatubo eruption the SO₂ cloud had an e-folding lifetime of about 3–5 weeks, and the resulting aerosol layer decayed with an e-folding time of about 12 months. Tropical eruptions spread aerosol into both hemispheres via the Brewer–Dobson circulation; high-latitude eruptions mainly affect their own hemisphere and have shorter aerosol lifetimes. Because the reaction is limited by OH availability, very large SO₂ injections may form larger particles that settle faster and scatter less efficiently per unit mass, a non-linearity that is important for super-eruptions.

Radiative effects

Sulfate aerosols scatter incoming shortwave radiation back to space, cooling the surface, while absorbing outgoing longwave and near-infrared radiation, warming the lower stratosphere. The net top-of-atmosphere effective radiative forcing scales approximately linearly with stratospheric aerosol optical depth (AOD) at 550 nm, with a coefficient of roughly −20 to −25 W m⁻² per unit AOD in recent assessments. Pinatubo injected roughly 15–20 Tg of SO₂, producing a peak global-mean AOD of about 0.1–0.15 and a peak forcing of roughly −3 W m⁻²; global mean surface temperature fell by about 0.4–0.5 °C in 1992. Paradoxically, winter warming over northern Eurasia and North America is often observed after large tropical eruptions, attributed to a strengthened polar vortex and positive North Atlantic Oscillation, though the robustness of this response is debated. Volcanic aerosols also provide surfaces for heterogeneous chemistry that accelerates ozone depletion, as observed after Pinatubo.

Ice cores and the volcanic record

Sulfate and tephra preserved in Greenland and Antarctic ice cores record past eruptions. Bipolar sulfate spikes indicate tropical eruptions; their magnitude allows estimation of stratospheric sulfur loading. Ice-core reconstructions (e.g. Sigl et al., 2015) show that clusters of large eruptions, such as those of the 530s–540s CE and the 1250s–1450s CE, coincide with cold periods, crop failures and societal stress recorded in tree rings and history. The 1257 eruption of Samalas (Rinjani complex, Lombok, Indonesia) produced the largest sulfate signal of the last two millennia. Volcanic forcing is a major contributor to natural climate variability over the past millennium and one of the dominant drivers of the onset of the Little Ice Age.

Water vapour and the Hunga Tonga anomaly

The 2022 Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai eruption injected comparatively little SO₂ (around 0.4–0.5 Tg) but about 150 Tg of water vapour — some 10% of the stratosphere's total water burden — a type of perturbation never previously observed. Water vapour is a greenhouse gas, and its prolonged residence in the stratosphere (several years) exerts a warming influence, while the sulfate aerosol exerted a cooling one; most studies find the net effect on global surface temperature to be small, and several find it slightly negative (net cooling), so the eruption is not considered a significant cause of the record warmth of 2023–2024. Quantification remains an active research topic. The eruption also affected stratospheric chemistry and circulation, reminding us that not all volcanic climate effects are cooling.

Volcanic CO₂ in context

Volcanoes emit CO₂ continuously, but the total from subaerial and submarine volcanism (including diffuse degassing) is estimated at a few hundred million tonnes per year — about 1% or less of anthropogenic emissions (~37 Gt CO₂ per year from fossil fuels and industry). On geological timescales, however, volcanic CO₂ is the main source of atmospheric carbon, and massive flood-basalt provinces (Siberian Traps at ~252 Ma; Central Atlantic Magmatic Province at ~201 Ma; Deccan Traps at ~66 Ma) are implicated in mass extinctions through greenhouse warming, ocean acidification and sulfur-driven cooling pulses.

Lesson 9: Volcanic Ash and Aviation
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Volcanic ash melts in jet engines, abrades aircraft, and drifts invisibly for thousands of kilometres; a global network of nine advisory centres tracks it

Why ash is dangerous to aircraft

Volcanic ash consists of angular fragments of glass, crystals and rock, hard enough (Mohs hardness ~5–6) to abrade windscreens, leading edges and sensors. More critically, the glass in ash softens and melts at roughly 1,000–1,100 °C, below the operating temperature of modern jet-engine combustors (which commonly exceed ~1,400 °C). Molten ash deposits on turbine nozzle guide vanes, restricting airflow and causing engine surge and flameout. Ash also blocks pitot tubes (corrupting airspeed indications) and contaminates cabin air. Volcanic clouds are often hard to distinguish from meteorological clouds at night or in cloud, and onboard weather radar detects only large particles.

Notable encounters

On 24 June 1982, British Airways Flight 9, a Boeing 747, flew into ash from Galunggung (Java) at about 37,000 ft. All four engines flamed out; the aircraft glided to about 13,500 ft before the crew restarted the engines and landed in Jakarta with abraded windscreens. On 15 December 1989, KLM Flight 867, a new Boeing 747-400, lost all four engines in ash from Redoubt (Alaska), descended several thousand metres before restarting, and landed in Anchorage with about US$80 million in damage. Between the 1970s and 2010s, more than a hundred ash encounters were documented, several dozen causing significant damage. No fatal crash has been attributed to ash, but the risk of catastrophe led to the creation of an international warning system.

The International Airways Volcano Watch and VAACs

Under the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) International Airways Volcano Watch, nine Volcanic Ash Advisory Centres (VAACs) — Anchorage, Buenos Aires, Darwin, London, Montréal, Tokyo, Toulouse, Washington and Wellington — monitor their regions using satellite remote sensing (visible, infrared split-window brightness temperature differences, and SO₂ retrievals), pilot reports and observatory notifications, and run dispersion models (for example NAME at London VAAC, HYSPLIT at Washington and other centres) to issue Volcanic Ash Advisories and graphical forecasts of ash extent at multiple flight levels. Volcano observatories issue Volcano Observatory Notices for Aviation (VONAs) using the aviation colour code. The quality of an ash forecast depends crucially on the eruption source parameters — plume height, mass eruption rate, vertical mass distribution, and the fraction of fine ash that survives near-source fallout (often only a few per cent) — which are themselves uncertain by an order of magnitude.

Eyjafjallajökull 2010 and concentration thresholds

The April–May 2010 eruption of Eyjafjallajökull (Iceland), a modest VEI 4 event fragmented by interaction with glacial meltwater, produced fine ash carried by persistent northwesterly winds over northern Europe. Under the existing policy of zero tolerance of ash, much of European airspace closed between 15 and 20 April; about 100,000 flights were cancelled, around 10 million passengers were affected, and airlines lost an estimated US$1.7 billion (IATA). The crisis prompted the rapid introduction of quantitative ash-concentration limits. European regulators defined zones of low (< 2 × 10⁻³ g m⁻³), medium (2–4 × 10⁻³ g m⁻³) and high (> 4 × 10⁻³ g m⁻³) contamination, with operations allowed in some zones subject to airlines' safety cases. This shift from avoidance to risk management demanded quantitative forecasts of concentration, which remain far less certain than forecasts of ash presence, and fuelled research on ensemble modelling, data assimilation and engine tolerance testing.

Settling of fine ash

Small particles settle slowly and can remain aloft for days. For particles much smaller than ~100 μm, the Stokes law gives a terminal velocity:

        v_{t} = (ρ_{p} − ρ_{a}) g d² / (18 μ)

where μ is the dynamic viscosity of air (~1.5 × 10⁻⁵ Pa s in the upper troposphere, though temperature-dependent). Non-sphericity reduces settling velocities further, while aggregation increases them.

Lesson 10: Hazard Mapping and Risk Reduction
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability; volcanic risk is reduced most effectively by land-use planning, monitoring, warning and evacuation, informed by clear communication

From hazard to risk

Volcanic hazard is the probability that a given phenomenon of a given intensity affects a location in a given time window; exposure is what is in harm's way (people, buildings, infrastructure, livelihoods); and vulnerability is the probability of loss given exposure, depending on building type, the intensity of the phenomenon (e.g. dynamic pressure, temperature, ash load) and the preparedness of people. Risk combines all three, and is commonly expressed as annual expected loss or as individual risk (annual probability of death at a location). Worldwide, the Global Volcano Model consortium and GVP estimate that roughly 800 million people live within 100 km of a Holocene volcano, and that a small number of countries (Indonesia, the Philippines, Japan, Mexico, Ethiopia, Guatemala) account for most of the exposed population.

Hazard maps

Traditional hazard maps are based on the geological history of the volcano: mapping past deposits, dating them, and assuming that future eruptions will resemble past ones. Modern maps increasingly combine geology with numerical modelling and probability: tephra fall from thousands of simulations using historical wind fields (e.g. with Tephra2 or Fall3D), PDC inundation probabilities from energy-cone or depth-averaged models (e.g. Titan2D, IMEX_SfloW2D), and lahar inundation from empirical methods. A widely used semi-empirical tool is LAHARZ (Iverson, Schilling and Vallance, 1998), based on analysis of 27 lahar paths at 9 volcanoes, which relates lahar volume V to the inundated cross-sectional area A and planimetric area B:

        A = 0.05 V^{2/3},     B = 200 V^{2/3}

These relations are applied with digital elevation models for a range of hypothetical volumes, yielding nested inundation zones. Probabilistic maps must clearly express uncertainty, and hazard maps are only effective when they are understood and used — the Nevado del Ruiz map of October 1985 correctly showed Armero in the lahar path, but was not acted upon in time.

Risk-reduction strategies

Land-use planning is the most effective long-term measure, but is often politically difficult on fertile volcanic soils with high population density; Italy's evacuation plan for Vesuvius, covering ~600,000–700,000 people in the "red zone", is a prominent example of planning for low-probability, high-consequence scenarios. Engineering measures include lahar dikes and sabo dams (widely used in Japan and Indonesia), roofs designed for ash loads, lava-diversion barriers, and degassing of lakes. Monitoring and early warning, including automated lahar-detection systems (acoustic flow monitors and tripwires on Mount Rainier and elsewhere), allow evacuation. Effective communication requires trust between scientists, authorities and communities; the success at Pinatubo owed much to an effective videotape that showed officials what pyroclastic flows and lahars could do. The 1986 creation of the USGS–USAID Volcano Disaster Assistance Program (VDAP), a direct response to Armero, has since supported crisis responses at dozens of volcanoes worldwide.

Statistics of fatalities

Auker et al. (2013) found that pyroclastic density currents are responsible for roughly one-third of recorded volcanic fatalities since 1600, with lahars, tsunamis, and indirect effects (famine and disease) also major contributors, and that most PDC fatalities occur within about 10 km of the vent, though lahars and tsunamis extend lethal reach to many tens of kilometres. The number of fatalities per event has fallen over time in regions with monitoring, but growing populations around volcanoes keep exposure increasing.

Open questions

Current research targets include the physics of magma mush remobilisation and the timescales over which eruptible magma accumulates (from diffusion chronometry of crystals, suggesting days to centuries); improved eruption forecasting using machine learning on multiparameter data; the long-term hazard of super-eruptions; better ensemble forecasting of ash dispersion; and the cascading impacts of eruptions on globalised systems (supply chains, telecommunications cables, aviation, food security) — the "volcanic black swan" scenario of a future VEI 7 eruption, for which humanity is poorly prepared.

Case Studies
═════════════════════════════════════════════

The eruptions below have shaped modern volcanology. Each illustrates a distinct combination of mechanism and impact, and each changed scientific understanding or practice. Figures quoted are those of the main scientific syntheses; where they are uncertain or disputed, ranges are given.

AD 79 Vesuvius, Italy — the archetypal Plinian eruption

The eruption of Vesuvius (then the volcano known as Somma–Vesuvius) followed centuries of dormancy and a destructive earthquake in AD 62/63. The traditional date of 24 August comes from manuscript copies of Pliny the Younger's letters to the historian Tacitus, but archaeological evidence — autumn fruits, heavier clothing, and a charcoal inscription found at Pompeii in 2018 — suggests a date around 24 October is more likely; the question remains debated.

The eruption began with a phreatomagmatic opening phase, then a Plinian phase in which a column of about 26–33 km dispersed phonolitic pumice to the south-east. Pompeii, ~10 km from the vent, was buried beneath 2–3 m of pumice fall over roughly 18 hours, which caused roofs to collapse. The column then became unstable and collapsed, sending a series of pyroclastic density currents across the region; at Herculaneum, only ~7 km to the west, the currents killed people sheltering in boathouses on the shore (the remains of more than 300 people were found there from the 1980s onwards) and buried the town under ~20 m of deposits. Total erupted volume is estimated at a few km³ DRE. The total death toll is unknown; more than 1,100 bodies have been recovered at Pompeii, and estimates of total fatalities across the region run to a few thousand or more. Pliny the Elder, commander of the fleet at Misenum, died at Stabiae while attempting a rescue.

Pliny the Younger's description of the column, "like an umbrella pine", gave its name to the Plinian style, and stratigraphic studies of the deposits by Sigurdsson, Carey and colleagues in the 1980s became a textbook example of column collapse and PDC dynamics. Vesuvius last erupted in 1944, and its red zone today contains ~600,000–700,000 people, making it one of the world's highest-risk volcanoes.

1783–1784 Laki, Iceland — a flood-basalt eruption in miniature

Between 8 June 1783 and 7 February 1784, a 27-km-long fissure system of about 140 vents (Lakagígar) erupted roughly 14.7 km³ of basaltic lava covering ~600 km², in ten eruptive episodes each beginning with explosive activity and lava fountains. The eruption released an estimated 122 Tg of SO₂ (Thordarson and Self, 2003), about 95 Tg of it into the upper troposphere and lower stratosphere, as well as large quantities of fluorine and chlorine.

In Iceland, fluorine poisoning of grass and the effects of the acid haze killed about 50% of cattle and horses and roughly 80% of sheep; the ensuing "Haze Famine" (Móðuharðindin) killed about 20–25% of the population (roughly 9,000–10,000 people). A sulfuric "dry fog" spread across Europe during summer 1783; contemporary accounts describe respiratory illness, crop damage and unusual heat, followed by a severe winter in 1783–1784. Studies of parish registers in England suggest excess mortality of the order of 10,000–20,000 people in summer and autumn 1783, plausibly linked to air pollution, although attribution is uncertain.

Laki demonstrates that effusive, low-VEI eruptions can have extreme atmospheric and societal impact. A similar eruption today would produce sustained air-quality hazards over Europe and major disruption of aviation and agriculture, which is why the UK has included a Laki-type eruption on its National Risk Register. Smaller, analogous effects occurred during the 2014–2015 Holuhraun eruption (Bárðarbunga), which released ~11 Tg of SO₂.

1815 Tambora, Indonesia — the largest eruption in recorded history

Tambora, on Sumbawa, reawakened in 1812 after centuries of quiescence. A preliminary explosion occurred on 5 April 1815; the climax on 10–11 April produced Plinian columns of up to ~43 km, then collapsed into pyroclastic density currents that swept to the sea, destroying the small kingdoms of Tambora and Sanggar. The summit lost ~1.5 km of height (from an estimated ~4,300 m to ~2,850 m) and a caldera about 6 km wide and ~1.1 km deep formed. Estimates of erupted volume range from ~30–33 km³ DRE (Sigurdsson and Carey, 1989) to ~41 km³ DRE (Kandlbauer and Sparks, 2014), placing it at VEI 7 and magnitude ~7. Explosions were heard more than 2,000 km away.

Direct deaths are estimated at about 10,000–12,000; famine and disease on Sumbawa and Lombok raised the total to perhaps 70,000–90,000 or more (Oppenheimer, 2003). Stratospheric sulfur injection is estimated at roughly 50–60 Tg of SO₂ — about three times Pinatubo — and the following year, 1816, became the "Year Without a Summer" in Europe and North America: snow fell in New England in June, harvests failed widely, grain prices soared and famine and typhus spread in parts of Europe. Northern Hemisphere summer temperatures were perhaps 0.4–0.7 °C below average, with much larger regional anomalies.

Tambora demonstrated the global reach of large eruptions, though the link between the eruption and 1816 weather was established only in the 20th century. It is a key benchmark for the effect of a future VEI 7 eruption on a modern, globalised society.

1883 Krakatau, Indonesia — the sound and the sea

Krakatau, in the Sunda Strait between Java and Sumatra, became active in May 1883. The climactic phase on 26–27 August culminated in four great explosions on the morning of 27 August, the largest at about 10:02 local time. The explosion was heard about 4,800 km away on Rodrigues Island in the Indian Ocean, and its atmospheric pressure wave was recorded by barographs around the world for several days as it circled the globe repeatedly. Most of the island collapsed into a submarine caldera. Erupted volume estimates are of order 10–20 km³ of bulk tephra (VEI 6), although they are uncertain.

Tsunamis, with run-up locally exceeding 30 m, devastated the coasts of the Sunda Strait and killed most of the roughly 36,000 victims (the official Dutch figure was 36,417). PDCs travelled across ~40 km of sea to reach Sumatra, killing perhaps a thousand or more people. The mechanism of the tsunamis — PDCs entering the sea, caldera collapse, or phreatomagmatic explosions — is still debated. Aerosols caused vivid red sunsets around the world and a measurable global cooling of a few tenths of a degree.

The Royal Society report of 1888 assembled worldwide observations, making Krakatau the first eruption studied as a global geophysical event, aided by the new telegraph network. Anak Krakatau ("child of Krakatau") emerged in 1927, and its flank collapse on 22 December 2018 generated a tsunami that killed more than 400 people, underscoring the persistence of volcano-tsunami hazard.

1980 Mount St. Helens, USA — the lateral blast

Mount St. Helens awoke on 20 March 1980 with an M4.2 earthquake, followed by phreatic explosions. Intrusion of a cryptodome of dacitic magma caused the north flank to bulge outward at up to ~1.5–2.5 m per day, displacing it by more than 140 m. At 8:32 a.m. PDT on 18 May, an earthquake of magnitude ~5.1 triggered the collapse of the bulge as a debris avalanche of ~2.5 km³, the largest landslide in recorded history. Sudden unloading depressurised the cryptodome and hydrothermal system, producing a lateral blast that devastated ~600 km² of forest, reaching speeds over 480 km/h (estimates range up to ~1,000 km/h). A Plinian column then rose to ~24 km and continued for about nine hours, while PDCs and lahars, especially down the Toutle River, destroyed bridges, homes and roads.

The eruption killed 57 people, including volcanologist David Johnston at an observation post ~10 km north of the vent. Total erupted volume (excluding the avalanche) was about 1 km³ bulk (VEI 5), and the summit lost ~400 m of height. Ash fell over eleven US states. The total economic loss was estimated at about US$1.1 billion (1980 dollars).

Mount St. Helens revolutionised understanding of edifice collapse and lateral blasts, and led to recognition that many volcanoes worldwide are built over prehistoric collapse deposits (hummocky terrain). It drove the expansion of the USGS Volcano Hazards Program and the Cascades Volcano Observatory, and its later dome-building phases (1980–1986, 2004–2008) became testing grounds for forecasting methods.

1985 Nevado del Ruiz, Colombia — a preventable catastrophe

Nevado del Ruiz, a glacier-capped stratovolcano in the Colombian Andes, showed unrest throughout 1985, including a phreatic eruption in September. A hazard map completed in early October correctly showed that lahars could reach Armero, a town in the valley of the Lagunillas River. On the evening of 13 November 1985, a small Plinian eruption (VEI 3, a few tens of million m³ of magma) began at about 21:08 local time. PDCs and ballistic ejecta scoured and melted part of the summit ice cap — perhaps 10% of its area — producing meltwater that generated lahars which swept down several drainages. Gaining volume by erosion, the lahars reached Armero, some 70 km downstream, at around 23:30, more than two hours after the eruption began. About 23,000 people were killed, most of them in Armero, where roughly three-quarters of the inhabitants died.

Failures of communication, uncertain responsibility, the absence of a clear chain of command, and reluctance to order an evacuation that might prove unnecessary combined to turn a moderate eruption into the second-deadliest volcanic disaster of the 20th century. The analysis by Voight (1990) remains a classic study of the "human factors" of volcanic crises.

Armero transformed international volcanology. It led to the creation of the USGS–USAID Volcano Disaster Assistance Program in 1986, the development of acoustic flow monitors for lahars, and an emphasis on risk communication, hazard education and scientist–authority relationships that proved decisive at Pinatubo six years later.

1991 Pinatubo, Philippines — a forecasting success

Pinatubo, on Luzon, had not erupted for about 500 years and was not widely recognised as dangerous. Phreatic explosions on 2 April 1991 prompted the Philippine Institute of Volcanology and Seismology (PHIVOLCS) and the USGS to install seismometers, tiltmeters and gas-measuring equipment. SO₂ emissions rose from ~500 t/day in May to over 5,000 t/day by late May, then fell — suggesting sealing of the system — while seismicity migrated beneath the summit and a dome appeared on 7 June. A five-level alert system was used, and evacuation zones were progressively extended to 20 and then 30 km, eventually involving some 60,000 local residents and about 15,000 personnel of the US Clark Air Base.

The climactic eruption on 15 June 1991 lasted about nine hours, coinciding with Typhoon Yunya. It produced a column of ~35 km (reaching ~40 km at its peak), an umbrella cloud several hundred kilometres across, and ignimbrites that filled valleys to depths of up to ~200 m. It erupted ~8–10 km³ of bulk tephra (~3.7–5.3 km³ DRE; Newhall and Punongbayan, 1996) and formed a caldera ~2.5 km wide. About 300–350 people died in the eruption, mostly from roof collapses under typhoon-soaked ash, and several hundred more in subsequent lahars and from disease in evacuation camps; the USGS estimated that forecasts and evacuations saved at least 5,000 lives and at least US$250 million in property. Lahars remobilised fresh deposits for more than a decade, burying towns such as Bacolor.

Pinatubo's ~15–20 Tg of SO₂ produced the most closely observed volcanic aerosol cloud in history. Global surface temperature fell by about 0.4–0.5 °C in 1992; predictions made by climate models before the cooling occurred (Hansen et al., 1992) provided an important test of climate sensitivity.

2022 Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai, Tonga — a submarine giant

Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai is a largely submarine caldera volcano whose rim had formed two small islands, joined by new land in a Surtseyan eruption in 2014–2015. After weeks of activity, a cataclysmic explosive eruption on 15 January 2022 — the climax beginning at about 04:15 UTC — generated a plume that reached ~57 km, into the mesosphere, the highest ever recorded by satellite. Detected global atmospheric waves included a Lamb wave that travelled around the Earth several times at ~310–320 m s⁻¹ and was recorded on barometers worldwide, much like Krakatau in 1883, and associated ionospheric disturbances. Lightning flash rates were the highest ever observed in any storm.

Tsunamis devastated parts of Tonga and were recorded across the Pacific and beyond, including waves arriving earlier than conventional tsunami travel times would predict, generated by coupling of the atmospheric pressure wave to the ocean. Volcanic density currents on the seafloor, travelling at ~120 km/h, destroyed telecommunications cables, cutting Tonga off from the world for weeks. Remarkably, deaths were few: about four in Tonga and two in Peru, where people drowned in waves on the coast ~10,000 km away. The eruption is usually rated VEI 5–6; seabed surveys show the caldera floor deepened by hundreds of metres and that several cubic kilometres of material were displaced.

The eruption injected unprecedented amounts of water into the stratosphere (~150 Tg; Millán et al., 2022) but little SO₂. It has prompted new research into submarine explosive volcanism, atmosphere–ocean coupling of tsunamis, and the vulnerability of undersea infrastructure.
