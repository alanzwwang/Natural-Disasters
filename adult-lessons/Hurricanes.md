Introduction
─────────────────────────────────

Tropical cyclones are among the most destructive weather systems on Earth. They are, with droughts, the deadliest meteorological hazard of the past half-century, they dominate the insured-loss record of the United States, the Caribbean, East Asia and Australia, and they are the principal source of coastal flooding in many low-lying deltas. In the North Atlantic and the eastern and central North Pacific they are called hurricanes, in the western North Pacific typhoons, and in the Indian Ocean and the Southern Hemisphere cyclones or tropical cyclones; physically they are the same phenomenon: a warm-core, non-frontal, synoptic-scale low-pressure system that draws its energy from the thermodynamic disequilibrium between a warm tropical ocean and the overlying atmosphere.

Hurricanes are also one of the clearest examples in geophysics of a natural heat engine. A mature storm extracts enthalpy from the sea surface at temperatures near 300 K, carries it upward in eyewall convection, and exports it to the upper troposphere and lower stratosphere at temperatures near 200 K. The mechanical work produced by this cycle is spent against surface friction, and the balance between energy input and dissipation sets an upper bound on intensity — the potential intensity — that can be computed from routine ocean and atmospheric soundings. That theoretical structure, developed largely from the 1980s onward, connects closely with operational practice: the categories issued by forecast centres, the surge and rainfall models used for evacuation, and the projections of how storms respond to a warming climate.

This course treats tropical cyclones at a graduate and professional level. It starts with climatology and genesis, develops the thermodynamic and dynamical theory of the mature vortex, and then turns to the hazards that kill people and destroy property — wind, storm surge and freshwater flooding — and to the observing systems, numerical models and forecast products used to anticipate them. The final lessons address climate change and attribution, and the engineering, planning and social science of risk reduction. A set of case studies from 1900 to 2022 illustrates how each component of the hazard has caused disasters, and what was learned from each.

In this course, you will:

  1. Explain the environmental conditions required for tropical cyclogenesis and interpret genesis indices
  2. Derive the Carnot-cycle view of a hurricane and compute potential intensity from sea-surface and outflow temperatures
  3. Describe the three-dimensional structure of the vortex and apply gradient, cyclostrophic and angular-momentum arguments
  4. Use intensity scales, pressure–wind relationships, satellite and reconnaissance methods, and understand their uncertainties
  5. Quantify storm surge from wind setup and the inverse-barometer effect, and relate it to bathymetry and coastal geometry
  6. Estimate storm rainfall totals from rain rate and translation speed, and explain inland flooding risk
  7. Interpret track and intensity forecasts, ensembles, the cone of uncertainty and rapid-intensification guidance
  8. Evaluate the evidence for climate-change effects on tropical cyclones and the methods of event attribution
  9. Assess wind engineering, evacuation planning, warning systems and financial risk-transfer as risk-reduction tools

Lesson 1: Climatology and Tropical Cyclogenesis
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Genesis needs warm, deep ocean water, a moist and conditionally unstable troposphere, weak vertical wind shear, sufficient planetary vorticity and a pre-existing disturbance

Global climatology

Roughly 80–90 tropical cyclones reaching tropical-storm strength (1-minute or 10-minute sustained winds of at least 34 knots, about 17.5 m/s) form worldwide each year, and the long-term mean is close to 85. The interannual standard deviation of the global count is small, roughly 8–10 % of the mean, which is a striking observation in its own right: global genesis appears to be regulated more tightly than basin counts are. About one-third of global activity occurs in the western North Pacific (roughly 26 tropical storms per year), followed by the eastern North Pacific (about 15–17), the South Indian Ocean and Australian region together (about 20–25), the North Atlantic (about 14 in the 1991–2020 climatology, higher than earlier periods partly because of better detection of short-lived storms), the South Pacific (about 7–9) and the North Indian Ocean (about 4–5, split between the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea). Tropical cyclones are almost absent from the South Atlantic and the south-east Pacific, where sea-surface temperatures are lower and vertical shear is strong; Cyclone Catarina (March 2004), which struck southern Brazil, is the best-documented exception.

Seasonality follows the ocean heat content. In the North Atlantic the official season runs from 1 June to 30 November with a sharp peak around 10 September; in the western North Pacific storms occur in every month with a broad maximum from July to October; the North Indian Ocean has a bimodal season (May and October–November) because the summer monsoon brings strong vertical shear between them; and Southern Hemisphere activity peaks from January to March.

Gray's seasonal genesis parameters

In a landmark 1968 survey, William Gray identified six large-scale conditions that together explain where and when genesis occurs. Three are thermodynamic: (1) ocean temperatures above about 26.5 °C through a mixed layer of order 50 m, so that storm-induced mixing does not immediately bring cold water to the surface; (2) a conditionally unstable atmosphere, i.e. a lapse rate that allows moist convection to reach the upper troposphere; and (3) high relative humidity in the lower and middle troposphere (roughly 700–500 hPa), which limits the entrainment of dry air that would otherwise generate cold downdrafts. Three are dynamical: (4) a latitude of more than about 5° from the equator, because the Coriolis parameter f = 2Ω sin φ vanishes at the equator and a balanced vortex cannot easily spin up there; (5) pre-existing low-level relative vorticity, supplied by easterly waves, the monsoon trough, decaying fronts or mesoscale convective systems; and (6) weak vertical shear of the horizontal wind between roughly 850 and 200 hPa, typically less than about 10–12 m/s.

        f = 2Ω sin φ        Ω = 7.292 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹

The 26.5 °C figure is not a hard physical threshold. In modern theory what matters is the thermodynamic disequilibrium between the ocean and the overlying atmosphere, which is captured by the potential intensity (Lesson 2) rather than by sea-surface temperature alone. The empirical threshold works in the present climate because the tropical troposphere is horizontally homogeneous in temperature (weak temperature gradients enforced by gravity waves), so a local sea-surface temperature above the tropical mean implies a local excess of surface enthalpy over the free troposphere. In a warmer climate the threshold for genesis is expected to rise along with the tropical mean temperature.

Seed disturbances and genesis pathways

Most North Atlantic tropical storms, and the great majority (roughly 85 %) of major hurricanes, develop from African easterly waves: synoptic-scale troughs with wavelengths of 2,000–3,000 km and periods of 3–5 days that propagate westward from West Africa. Roughly 60 waves leave the African coast each season, but only a minority become tropical storms. In the western North Pacific, the monsoon trough, monsoon gyres and the shear lines of the intertropical convergence zone supply most seed vorticity. A minority of storms, particularly at higher latitudes, form through "tropical transition" of an upper-level cold low or a decaying baroclinic system.

The transition from a cluster of convection to a self-sustaining warm-core vortex remains one of the least well understood steps in tropical meteorology. Current conceptual models emphasise the need for a protected, moist mid-level environment in which convection can moisten the column and build a mid-level vortex; the "marsupial pouch" hypothesis of Dunkerton, Montgomery and Wang (2009) describes a region of closed circulation in the frame moving with an easterly wave that shelters the developing core from dry environmental air. Vortical hot towers, small intense convective updrafts that stretch and concentrate vorticity, are thought to contribute to the spin-up of the low-level circulation. Field programmes such as PREDICT (2010) and NASA's GRIP and HS3 campaigns were designed to test these ideas.

Genesis potential indices

Empirical genesis indices combine the environmental factors into a single number that reproduces the observed spatial and seasonal distribution of genesis. The widely used Genesis Potential Index of Emanuel and Nolan (2004) is

        GPI = |10⁵ η|^{3/2} (H/50)³ (V_{pot}/70)³ (1 + 0.1 V_{shear})^{-2}

where η is the absolute vorticity at 850 hPa (s⁻¹), H the relative humidity at 600 hPa (%), V_{pot} the potential intensity (m/s) and V_{shear} the magnitude of the 850–200 hPa vertical shear (m/s). Indices of this type reproduce the climatological seasonal cycle well and capture some of the response of basin activity to ENSO, but they are not reliable predictors of how global storm frequency will change in a warmer climate, because their empirical form was fitted to the present climate (Lesson 9).

Modes of variability

The El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is the dominant source of interannual variability. During El Niño, enhanced upper-level westerlies over the Caribbean and tropical Atlantic increase vertical shear and suppress Atlantic activity, while genesis in the western North Pacific shifts eastward and storms there tend to be longer-lived and more intense; La Niña reverses these tendencies. The Madden–Julian Oscillation modulates genesis on intraseasonal (30–60 day) time scales, and multidecadal variability of Atlantic sea-surface temperature (often called the Atlantic Multidecadal Variability) is associated with active (1950s–1960s, 1995 onward) and inactive (1970–1994) Atlantic eras, although the relative roles of internal ocean variability and of changes in anthropogenic and volcanic aerosols in those eras are actively debated.

Lesson 2: The Hurricane as a Heat Engine and Potential Intensity
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        V_{max}² ≈ (C_{k}/C_{D}) · [(T_{s} − T_{o})/T_{o}] · (k₀* − k)

The energy source

The energy of a tropical cyclone comes from the transfer of enthalpy — overwhelmingly latent heat in the form of water vapour, with a smaller sensible-heat contribution — from the ocean to the atmosphere. The tropical boundary layer is normally undersaturated with respect to the sea surface: air in the boundary layer is cooler and drier than the saturated air in contact with the water. In the high winds of a hurricane core this disequilibrium drives large surface fluxes. The bulk aerodynamic formula for the enthalpy flux is

        F_{k} = ρ C_{k} |V| (k₀* − k)

where ρ is air density, C_{k} the enthalpy exchange coefficient, |V| the near-surface wind speed, k₀* the saturation specific enthalpy of air at the sea-surface temperature and pressure, and k the specific enthalpy of the boundary-layer air. Because the flux is proportional to wind speed, stronger winds draw more energy from the ocean — a positive feedback, termed wind-induced surface heat exchange (WISHE), that underpins the air–sea interaction theory of Emanuel (1986). The total rate of latent-heat release is enormous: a storm raining an average of 1.5 cm per day over a circle of radius 665 km releases about 6 × 10¹⁴ W, the figure Emanuel uses to illustrate that a hurricane's heat release is of order a hundred times humanity's total electrical generating capacity. Only a few per cent of this heat is converted to kinetic energy.

The Carnot cycle

Emanuel's idealised mature hurricane operates as a Carnot cycle with four legs. (1) Air spirals inward in the boundary layer at nearly constant temperature T_{s}, gaining enthalpy isothermally from the sea. (2) It ascends adiabatically in the eyewall, conserving moist entropy, to the outflow level. (3) It flows out at the tropopause and loses heat by infrared radiation at a nearly constant low temperature T_{o} (approximately 190–210 K). (4) It descends slowly far from the centre, compressing and losing entropy radiatively, to close the cycle. The thermodynamic efficiency of a Carnot engine is

        ε = (T_{s} − T_{o}) / T_{s}

and for T_{s} = 300 K and T_{o} = 200 K, ε ≈ 1/3. The mechanical energy generated is dissipated mostly by surface friction in the boundary layer near the radius of maximum winds. Bister and Emanuel (1998) showed that this frictional dissipation produces heat in the boundary layer that is itself recycled into the engine, which raises the effective efficiency so that T_{o} rather than T_{s} appears in the denominator.

Potential intensity

Balancing the rate of energy generation against the rate of frictional dissipation (proportional to ρ C_{D} |V|³) at the radius of maximum wind gives the potential intensity:

        V_{p}² = (C_{k}/C_{D}) · [(T_{s} − T_{o}) / T_{o}] · (k₀* − k)

V_{p} is the theoretical maximum gradient wind speed near the top of the boundary layer for a steady storm in a given environment. Over the tropical oceans in late summer, computed potential intensity is typically 60–85 m/s, and the theory reproduces the observed upper bound of storm intensity reasonably well: very few storms exceed their local potential intensity, although the strongest (Patricia in 2015, with about 185 kt or 95 m/s estimated) approach or locally exceed some estimates. Most storms never reach their potential intensity: the distribution of lifetime maximum intensity normalised by potential intensity is broad, with the median storm reaching roughly half of it, because shear, dry air, ocean cooling, land interaction and limited lifetime all intervene.

The exchange coefficients

The ratio C_{k}/C_{D} matters as much as the thermodynamics. Measurements at high wind speeds are extremely difficult, but GPS dropwindsonde profiles (Powell, Vickery and Reinhold, 2003) showed that the drag coefficient C_{D} stops increasing with wind speed above about 30–35 m/s and levels off at roughly 2–2.5 × 10⁻³, plausibly because sea spray and a "slip" layer of foam alter the momentum transfer. Field and laboratory work, including the CBLAST campaign (2003–2004), suggests that C_{k} remains roughly constant near 1.0–1.2 × 10⁻³, giving C_{k}/C_{D} of order 0.5–1. Emanuel's theory requires C_{k}/C_{D} of roughly 0.75 or more to explain observed intense storms, and the role of sea spray in enhancing enthalpy flux remains an open research question.

Ocean coupling

A hurricane cools the sea beneath it. Wind stress drives strong inertial currents in the ocean mixed layer, and the associated shear-driven turbulence entrains colder water from the thermocline; upwelling under the storm core adds to the effect. The resulting cold wake can be 1–6 °C cooler than the surrounding sea, and the cooling under a slow-moving storm can reduce its intensity substantially. For this reason the relevant thermodynamic quantity is often taken to be the tropical cyclone heat potential or ocean heat content (the integrated heat above the 26 °C isotherm, in kJ/cm²), or the temperature averaged over the upper 80–100 m, rather than the skin sea-surface temperature. Warm-core eddies shed by the Gulf of Mexico Loop Current, with deep warm layers, have been implicated in the rapid intensification of several storms, including Opal (1995), Katrina and Rita (2005).

Lesson 3: Structure and Dynamics of the Mature Vortex
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        V²/r + fV = (1/ρ) ∂p/∂r        (gradient-wind balance)

The primary and secondary circulations

A mature hurricane has two coupled circulations. The primary circulation is the swirling tangential flow — cyclonic (anticlockwise in the Northern Hemisphere, clockwise in the Southern) at low levels — with wind speed rising from near zero at the centre to a maximum at the radius of maximum winds (typically 15–60 km, occasionally under 10 km in the most intense small storms) and decaying outward roughly as r^{-0.5} to r^{-0.7}. The secondary circulation is the overturning flow in the radius–height plane: frictional inflow in the lowest 1–2 km, ascent in the eyewall, outflow in a shallow layer near the tropopause, and slow subsidence over a wide area outside the storm. The outflow is anticyclonic at upper levels, because air moving outward while conserving angular momentum loses cyclonic spin.

The eye, the eyewall and rainbands

The eye is a region of light winds and subsiding air, 10–60 km across in most storms. Subsidence warms the eye adiabatically and makes the storm warm-cored: temperature anomalies relative to the environment are typically 5–10 K in the middle and upper troposphere and can reach 15–20 K in the most intense storms. By hydrostatic balance this column of warm, light air explains the very low surface pressure at the centre. The eyewall is a ring of deep convection that slopes outward with height, following surfaces of constant absolute angular momentum; it contains the strongest winds and heaviest rain. Outside the eyewall, spiral rainbands extend hundreds of kilometres from the centre. Inner rainbands are linked to vortex Rossby waves propagating on the radial gradient of vorticity, while outer rainbands are closer in character to ordinary squall lines.

Gradient and cyclostrophic balance

Above the boundary layer, the tangential wind is close to gradient-wind balance: the inward pressure-gradient force balances the sum of the centrifugal and Coriolis forces.

        V²/r + fV = (1/ρ) ∂p/∂r

The relative importance of the two terms on the left is measured by the Rossby number Ro = V/(fr). Near the eyewall of an intense storm, with V = 50 m/s, r = 30 km and f = 5 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹, Ro ≈ 33, so the Coriolis term is negligible and the flow is nearly cyclostrophic: V² ≈ (r/ρ) ∂p/∂r. In the outer circulation, at several hundred kilometres radius where winds are weaker, Ro approaches unity and the Coriolis term matters. In the boundary layer, friction reduces the tangential wind below gradient balance, the pressure gradient is no longer fully balanced, and air accelerates inward — the physical origin of the frictional inflow.

Angular momentum

The absolute angular momentum per unit mass about the storm axis is defined as:

        M = rV + ½ f r²

and above the boundary layer it is approximately conserved by air parcels. If an air ring at 500 km radius with no relative motion were drawn inward to 50 km while conserving M, its tangential velocity would rise to over 120 m/s — far stronger than any observed storm. The real maximum is limited because M is lost to the sea surface by friction as air flows inward in the boundary layer, and because inflow ceases at the eyewall. Angular-momentum arguments also explain why the outflow becomes anticyclonic at large radii and why the eyewall slopes outward.

Thermal wind and the warm core

Because the core is warm, the radial pressure gradient decreases with height, and so does the tangential wind: the cyclonic circulation is strongest just above the boundary layer, near 0.5–1 km, and weakens upward until it reverses to anticyclonic outflow near the tropopause. In balanced-vortex theory (the Sawyer–Eliassen equation) the secondary circulation is the response required to maintain thermal-wind balance when heating (eyewall latent-heat release) and momentum sources (friction) act on the vortex.

Eyewall replacement cycles

Intense storms often develop a secondary, concentric eyewall at larger radius, separated from the original by a relatively cloud-free moat. The outer eyewall contracts and cuts off the inflow of moist, high-enthalpy air to the inner eyewall, which decays; the storm typically weakens during the cycle, then may reintensify as the new eyewall contracts. Willoughby, Clos and Shoreibah (1982) documented this behaviour from aircraft data, and it is a major source of intensity fluctuations over periods of 12–36 hours. A replacement cycle also broadens the wind field, enlarging the area exposed to hurricane-force winds and increasing the surge-generating potential even as the peak wind falls.

Asymmetries and the effect of motion

Real storms are asymmetric. The storm's translation adds to the wind on the right of the track in the Northern Hemisphere (left in the Southern), so the strongest winds and surge usually occur in the right-front quadrant relative to motion. Environmental vertical shear tilts the vortex and organises convection on the downshear-left side; strong shear ventilates the core with dry, low-entropy air and is the commonest cause of weakening over open water.

Lesson 4: Measuring and Classifying Intensity
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Intensity is defined by maximum sustained surface wind, but it is almost never measured directly; it is inferred from aircraft, satellite and pressure data with errors of order 10 %

Definitions and averaging periods

Intensity is conventionally defined as the maximum sustained wind at 10 m height over open, unobstructed exposure. The United States (National Hurricane Center, Central Pacific Hurricane Center and the Joint Typhoon Warning Center) uses a 1-minute averaging period, while the World Meteorological Organization's regional specialised meteorological centres — including the Japan Meteorological Agency, the India Meteorological Department, Météo-France La Réunion and the Australian Bureau of Meteorology — use a 10-minute average. The two are not interchangeable: WMO guidance (Harper, Kepert and Ginger, 2010) recommends a conversion factor of about 0.93 from 1-minute to 10-minute winds over the sea, rather than the older 0.88. Gusts (3-second peaks) are typically 1.2–1.3 times the 1-minute wind over water and larger over rough terrain. These conventions explain why the same storm often appears in official records with different peak winds; Typhoon Haiyan, for example, is listed at 170 kt (1-minute, JTWC) but 125 kt (10-minute, JMA).

Regional terminology

The terminology also differs. In the North Atlantic and the North Pacific east of the dateline, a tropical depression has winds below 34 kt, a tropical storm 34–63 kt and a hurricane 64 kt or more; a major hurricane is Category 3 or above (≥ 96 kt). In the western North Pacific, JMA uses tropical storm, severe tropical storm and typhoon, and JTWC applies "super typhoon" to storms of 130 kt or more (1-minute). The India Meteorological Department grades depression, deep depression, cyclonic storm, severe cyclonic storm, very severe cyclonic storm, extremely severe cyclonic storm and super cyclonic storm (≥ 120 kt, 3-minute average in IMD practice). The Australian Bureau of Meteorology uses its own five-category scale based on gusts.

The Saffir–Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale

The Saffir–Simpson scale was devised in the early 1970s by the engineer Herbert Saffir and the then-director of the National Hurricane Center, Robert Simpson. Originally it combined wind, central pressure and storm surge, but since 2010 the National Hurricane Center has used a wind-only version (the Saffir–Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale), because surge depends strongly on storm size and coastal bathymetry and is poorly predicted by peak wind. The category thresholds (1-minute sustained wind) are:

        Category   kt          mph         km/h
        1          64–82       74–95       119–153
        2          83–95       96–110      154–177
        3          96–112      111–129     178–208
        4          113–136     130–156     209–251
        5          ≥ 137       ≥ 157       ≥ 252

Because wind loads scale with V² and damage with a higher power, the jump in potential damage from Category 3 to 5 is several-fold. The scale says nothing about rainfall, surge, size or forward speed — which, historically, have caused most deaths — and NHC has repeatedly emphasised that a storm's category should not be used as a proxy for its total hazard.

Pressure–wind relationships

Central pressure is easier to observe than maximum wind (a dropsonde in the eye or a ground station gives it accurately) and it is a better integral measure of storm strength. Empirical pressure–wind relationships, such as that of Atkinson and Holliday (1977) for the western North Pacific,

        V_{max} (kt) = 6.7 (1010 − p_{c})^{0.644}        (p_{c} in hPa)

convert one to the other, but scatter around such relations is large because maximum wind also depends on storm size, latitude, environmental pressure and translation speed. Modern relationships (for example Knaff and Zehr, 2007) include these factors explicitly. The analytical profile of Holland (1980),

        p(r) = p_{c} + Δp exp[−(R_{max}/r)^{B}]

with Δp the pressure deficit and B a shape parameter typically 1–2.5, gives a maximum cyclostrophic wind of approximately V_{max} ≈ √(BΔp/(ρe)), and is widely used in surge and catastrophe models.

Aircraft reconnaissance

The most accurate intensity data come from aircraft. In the Atlantic, the US Air Force Reserve's 53rd Weather Reconnaissance Squadron flies WC-130J aircraft, and NOAA operates two WP-3D Orion research aircraft and a Gulfstream IV-SP that samples the steering environment at high altitude. Aircraft measure flight-level winds (typically at 700 hPa, about 3 km), release GPS dropwindsondes that profile wind, temperature, humidity and pressure down to the sea surface, and carry the stepped-frequency microwave radiometer (SFMR), which infers surface wind speed from the microwave emission of foam. Analysis of dropsonde profiles by Franklin, Black and Valde (2003) established that the surface wind in the eyewall averages about 90 % of the 700-hPa flight-level wind, a reduction factor still used operationally. Routine aircraft reconnaissance of the western North Pacific ended in 1987, so most typhoon intensities since then are satellite-based estimates; this has consequences for the homogeneity of the climate record (Lesson 9).

Satellite methods

The Dvorak technique (Dvorak, 1975, 1984) estimates intensity from visible and infrared cloud patterns. The analyst identifies a pattern type (curved band, central dense overcast, embedded centre or eye), measures features such as the curvature of the main band or the temperature difference between the warm eye and the cold surrounding cloud tops, and assigns a T-number between 1.0 and 8.0 that maps to a maximum wind (for example T4.0 ≈ 65 kt, T5.0 ≈ 90 kt, T6.0 ≈ 115 kt, T7.0 ≈ 140 kt and T8.0 ≈ 170 kt). Constraints limit how fast the T-number can change. The objective Advanced Dvorak Technique (ADT; Olander and Velden, 2007) automates this using infrared data. Satellite microwave sounders measure the upper-level warm anomaly (AMSU-based methods), passive microwave imagers at 37 and 85–91 GHz reveal eyewalls beneath cirrus, and scatterometers (ASCAT) and synthetic aperture radar map surface wind fields. Satellite-only intensity estimates carry typical uncertainties of 10–20 % in the absence of aircraft data.

Naming

Storms are named from lists maintained by the WMO regional committees. Atlantic names rotate on a six-year cycle, alternating male and female names since 1979; names of particularly deadly or costly storms (such as Katrina, Maria and Ian) are retired. In the western North Pacific, names are contributed by the members of the ESCAP/WMO Typhoon Committee and include many non-personal names. After the 2005 and 2020 seasons exhausted the Atlantic list, the Greek alphabet was used; since 2021 a supplemental list of names has replaced it.

Lesson 5: The Wind Hazard and Structural Engineering
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Wind pressure q = ½ρV² grows with the square of wind speed, and observed damage rises even faster — roughly with the cube or higher power of wind speed

From wind speed to load

The dynamic pressure exerted by wind of speed V on a surface normal to the flow is q = ½ρV². With ρ = 1.225 kg/m³, a wind of 50 m/s exerts about 1.5 kPa and one of 70 m/s about 3.0 kPa: a 40 % increase in wind speed nearly doubles the load. Real buildings experience pressures that are this reference value multiplied by pressure coefficients that depend on geometry, and the most damaging loads are suction (negative pressure) on roofs, at roof corners and edges and on leeward walls. Building codes such as ASCE 7 in the United States express the design velocity pressure as q_{z} = 0.613 K_{z} K_{zt} K_{d} V² (in N/m² with V in m/s), where K_{z} accounts for height and terrain exposure, K_{zt} for topographic speed-up and K_{d} for wind directionality. Design wind speeds are mapped as 3-second gusts at 10 m in open terrain, for specified return periods (for ordinary buildings in ASCE 7-16 and 7-22, a mean recurrence interval of 700 years).

Failure modes

Post-storm damage surveys after Hurricane Andrew (1992) and subsequent storms identified a consistent sequence of failures in light-frame construction: loss of roof covering and sheathing, especially at gable ends and corners; breach of windows and doors by wind-borne debris, which pressurises the interior and adds to the uplift on the roof; failure of the roof-to-wall connection; and, in the worst cases, collapse of walls. Engineered responses include hurricane straps and clips that create a continuous load path from roof to foundation, closer nailing of roof sheathing, sealed roof decks, impact-resistant glazing or shutters tested against standard missile impacts, and hip rather than gable roofs. The Florida Building Code, adopted statewide in 2002 largely as a result of Andrew, made many of these provisions mandatory; damage studies after Hurricane Charley (2004) and later storms found substantially lower losses in houses built to the post-2002 code.

Boundary-layer and terrain effects

Surface winds over land are reduced relative to winds over water by increased roughness, but gusts become relatively stronger. Topography produces speed-up over hills and escarpments, and the boundary layer of intense storms contains small-scale features — boundary-layer rolls and eyewall mesovortices — that can produce localised streaks of extreme damage. Tornadoes are common in the outer rainbands of landfalling storms, especially in the right-front quadrant; Hurricane Ivan (2004) produced more than 100 tornadoes in the United States.

Wind-field models and damage functions

Catastrophe models used by insurers and reinsurers simulate many thousands of synthetic storms, compute the wind field of each with parametric profiles such as Holland's (Lesson 4) and a boundary-layer model, apply terrain and roughness corrections, and translate local peak gusts into damage ratios using vulnerability (fragility) curves calibrated on claims data. Empirical studies of aggregated losses suggest that damage scales with wind speed to a power of three or higher; Emanuel's power dissipation index (Emanuel, 2005), PDI = ∫ V_{max}³ dt, was introduced as a climate metric for this reason. The kinetic energy dissipated by surface friction in a single mature storm, of order ρ C_{D} V³ integrated over the core area, is about 3 × 10¹² W for typical values.

Lesson 6: Storm Surge and Coastal Waves
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Δη_{wind} ≈ τ_{s} L / (ρ_{w} g h)        Δη_{pressure} ≈ Δp / (ρ_{w} g) ≈ 1 cm per hPa

What storm surge is

Storm surge is the abnormal rise of sea level generated by a storm, over and above the astronomical tide; storm tide is the total water level (surge plus tide). Historically, storm surge has been the deadliest component of tropical cyclones: it was the main cause of death in the 1970 Bhola cyclone, the 1991 Bangladesh cyclone, Cyclone Nargis (2008) and Typhoon Haiyan (2013), and, according to Rappaport's (2014) analysis of US fatalities from 1963 to 2012, about half of direct US tropical-cyclone deaths in that period were caused by surge. The surge is accompanied by wind waves riding on top of it, wave setup (a rise of mean level in the surf zone due to wave breaking) and, in some reef settings, long-period infragravity waves.

Wind setup

The dominant mechanism over a shallow continental shelf is wind setup. Onshore wind exerts a surface stress τ_{s} = ρ_{a} C_{D} U² on the sea. In a steady state with no flow through the coast, this stress is balanced by a seaward pressure gradient from the sloping water surface:

        ∂η/∂x = τ_{s} / [ρ_{w} g (h + η)]

where h is the water depth. The inverse dependence on depth is the key to coastal vulnerability: the same wind piles up a far larger surge over a broad, shallow shelf (the north-eastern Gulf of Mexico, the Bay of Bengal, the Irrawaddy delta) than over a narrow, steep one (much of the Pacific coast of Mexico or the east coast of Taiwan). Coastline shape also matters: funnel-shaped bays and estuaries concentrate water, and the Coriolis force deflects wind-driven currents and modifies the alongshore surge. Because τ_{s} scales with U², surge grows rapidly with wind speed, and because the setup integrates over the fetch, large storms produce larger surges than small storms of the same peak wind. The right-front quadrant (Northern Hemisphere), where storm motion and onshore wind combine, usually has the highest surge.

The inverse-barometer effect

The low pressure at the storm centre also raises the sea surface hydrostatically: a pressure deficit of Δp lifts the water by Δp/(ρ_{w} g), about 1 cm per hPa. For a deep storm with Δp = 60–100 hPa this contributes 0.6–1.0 m — important but usually much smaller than wind setup in shallow water. In deep water far from land it is the main component.

Forerunner surge, resurgence and bores

Coastal water levels often begin rising a day or more before landfall as shore-parallel winds drive Ekman transport toward the coast; this forerunner surge flooded Galveston Island hours before the 1900 hurricane arrived and was a major problem in Hurricane Ike (2008). Water can also be blown away from one coast (negative surge) and return violently when winds shift. Over coral reefs, short waves breaking at the reef edge can generate surf-beat — long infragravity waves — that propagate across the reef flat as a bore; Roeber and Bricker (2015) showed that this produced a tsunami-like wave at Hernani in Eastern Samar during Haiyan.

Surge modelling

Operational surge forecasting in the United States uses the SLOSH model (Sea, Lake and Overland Surges from Hurricanes), developed at NOAA from the late 1960s by Chester Jelesnianski and colleagues, which solves the depth-integrated shallow-water equations on basin-specific grids with parametric wind and pressure fields. SLOSH is fast enough to run hundreds of hypothetical storms: its MEOW (Maximum Envelope of Water) and MOM (Maximum of MEOWs) products drive evacuation zone mapping, and probabilistic surge guidance (P-Surge) combines ensembles of storms perturbed in track, size and intensity. Higher-resolution unstructured-mesh models such as ADCIRC, coupled with spectral wave models (SWAN, WAVEWATCH III), are used for engineering design, levee certification and research, and produced the hindcasts behind the post-Katrina reconstruction of the New Orleans flood defences.

Since 2017 the National Hurricane Center has issued explicit storm-surge watches and warnings, and it expresses surge forecasts as inundation above ground level rather than as elevation above a datum, because people need to know how deep the water will be where they stand.

Sea-level rise

Sea-level rise raises the base on which surges act, so that a given surge reaches further inland and overtops defences more often. Global mean sea level rose by about 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018 (IPCC AR6), and the rate has accelerated to around 3.7 mm per year over 2006–2018. Because flood frequency curves are often flat, a modest rise in base level can turn a once-a-century water level into an event occurring every few years.

Lesson 7: Rainfall and Inland Flooding
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Storm total rainfall ≈ (mean rain rate) × (rain-area diameter) / (translation speed)

Rain production

A tropical cyclone is an efficient converter of water vapour to precipitation. Rain rates in the eyewall commonly exceed 50 mm/h and storm-average rates within 100–200 km of the centre are of order 5–15 mm/h. The total rainfall at a point depends far more on how long the rain lasts there than on how intense the storm is: slow-moving and stalled storms produce the greatest totals, regardless of their wind category. Tropical Storm Allison (2001) flooded Houston without ever becoming a hurricane, and Tropical Storm Claudette (1979) dropped about 1,070 mm (42 in.) in 24 hours at Alvin, Texas. Several world-record point rainfalls, including those for 12 hours, 24 hours and 72 hours, were produced by tropical cyclones on La Réunion, where steep orography amplifies rain from slow-moving storms; Cyclone Denise (1966) produced about 1,825 mm in 24 hours at Foc-Foc.

Factors controlling rainfall

Four factors combine. Storm motion sets duration; storm size sets the width of the rain swath; environmental moisture (total precipitable water, typically 50–70 mm in the tropics) and storm intensity set the rain rate; and topography enhances rain on windward slopes and can lift moist air in otherwise weak systems, as in Hurricane Mitch (1998) over Honduras and Nicaragua and Typhoon Morakot (2009) over Taiwan, where more than 2,700 mm fell in about four days in the mountains. Interaction with fronts during extratropical transition can produce heavy rain far from the original circulation, as with Hurricane Floyd (1999) in North Carolina. Simple climatology-and-persistence rainfall models (R-CLIPER) capture the first-order dependence on motion and intensity; dynamical models now provide most operational guidance.

The Clausius–Clapeyron constraint

Saturation vapour pressure rises by about 7 % per kelvin of warming at tropical temperatures (the Clausius–Clapeyron relation). Because tropical cyclones converge moisture from a large area, their rain rates are expected to scale at roughly this rate or somewhat faster; model studies project increases in near-core rain rates of the order of 10–15 % for 2 °C of global warming (Knutson et al., 2020). Attribution studies of Hurricane Harvey (Lesson 9) found rainfall totals substantially larger than the Clausius–Clapeyron rate alone would suggest, though with wide uncertainty ranges.

Inland flooding and mortality

Freshwater flooding has become a leading cause of tropical-cyclone deaths in the United States. Rappaport (2014) found that rain-induced floods caused about a quarter of direct US deaths over 1963–2012, many of them in vehicles driven into flooded roads, and the proportion was much higher in years without major surge events. In Central America, the Caribbean and parts of Asia, rainfall-triggered flash floods, landslides and debris flows account for most deaths, as in Mitch (1998), Hurricane Jeanne in Haiti (2004, about 3,000 deaths, mostly in Gonaïves) and Hurricane Stan (2005) in Guatemala. Compound flooding — the simultaneous occurrence of surge and river or pluvial flooding — is a particular hazard in estuaries and urban drainage systems.

Translation speed trends

Kossin (2018) reported a global decrease of about 10 % in tropical-cyclone translation speed over 1949–2016, which would increase local rainfall totals, but subsequent studies (for example Moon, Kim and Chan, 2019, and Lanzante, 2019) questioned how much of the trend reflects changes in observing practice and natural variability. The trend in stalling over the US coastline remains an active research question.

Lesson 8: Track and Intensity Forecasting
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Track is governed by large-scale steering plus beta drift and has improved steadily; intensity, especially rapid intensification, is governed by inner-core and ocean processes and has improved more slowly

Steering and beta drift

To first order a tropical cyclone moves with the vertically averaged environmental flow, the "steering current", typically weighted over a deep layer (850–200 hPa for intense storms, shallower for weaker ones). Subtropical ridges steer storms westward in the deep tropics; storms recurve poleward and eastward around the western flank of the ridge and into the mid-latitude westerlies. Superimposed on steering is beta drift: the interaction of the vortex with the meridional gradient of planetary vorticity (β = ∂f/∂y) produces a pair of large-scale gyres (the "beta gyres") that advect the storm poleward and westward relative to the steering flow at about 1–3 m/s. Interactions between nearby storms (the Fujiwhara effect), with upper-level troughs and with the storm's own outflow modify the motion.

Numerical guidance

Modern track forecasts rely on global numerical weather prediction models — the ECMWF Integrated Forecasting System, NOAA's Global Forecast System, the UK Met Office Unified Model and others — and on regional hurricane models with moving nests at kilometre-scale resolution, such as NOAA's Hurricane Analysis and Forecast System (HAFS), which replaced HWRF operationally in 2023. Ensembles (for example the 51-member ECMWF ensemble) sample uncertainty in initial conditions and model physics, and forecasters routinely give heavy weight to consensus aids that average several models. Since 2023, machine-learning global models trained on reanalysis data (for example GraphCast, Pangu-Weather and ECMWF's AIFS) have shown track skill competitive with the best physics-based systems, and forecast centres have begun evaluating them operationally.

Track errors and the cone of uncertainty

Official NHC Atlantic track errors have declined by roughly 2–3 % per year for decades; today's 72-hour errors are smaller than 24-hour errors were in the early 1970s, and the mean 5-day error is now of the order of a few hundred kilometres. The NHC "cone of uncertainty" is constructed from a set of circles centred on each forecast position, with radii chosen so that two-thirds of official forecast errors over the previous five years fall within them. The cone therefore conveys only the probable path of the centre: by construction, the centre leaves the cone about one-third of the time, and the cone says nothing about the size of the storm or the extent of hazards — hurricane-force winds, surge and rain often extend far beyond it. Misreading the cone as a damage footprint is a recognised risk-communication problem.

Intensity forecasting

Intensity forecasts improved little from 1990 to about 2010, then more rapidly, aided by better models, aircraft data assimilation, statistical-dynamical schemes such as SHIPS (Statistical Hurricane Intensity Prediction Scheme) and LGEM, and consensus aids. Intensity depends on processes at scales of a few kilometres — eyewall convection, replacement cycles, interaction with ocean eddies and dry air — that are difficult to observe and simulate, and on accurate representation of air–sea fluxes at high winds.

Rapid intensification

Rapid intensification (RI) is conventionally defined as an increase of at least 30 kt in maximum sustained wind in 24 hours (Kaplan and DeMaria, 2003), which corresponds roughly to the 95th percentile of 24-hour intensity changes in the Atlantic. Kaplan and DeMaria found that about 83 % of Atlantic major hurricanes (and all Category 4–5 storms in their sample) underwent at least one RI episode, and RI shortly before landfall — as in Opal (1995), Charley (2004), Michael (2018), Ida (2021), Ian (2022) and Otis (2023), which intensified from tropical storm to Category 5 in about 24 hours before striking Acapulco — is among the most dangerous forecast failures because it leaves little time to change warnings. Favourable conditions include high potential intensity and ocean heat content, weak shear, a moist mid-troposphere and an already well-organised inner core. Probabilistic guidance such as the SHIPS rapid intensification index estimates the probability of RI given environmental predictors.

Warnings

In the United States, hurricane watches are issued when hurricane conditions are possible, generally 48 hours before tropical-storm-force winds are expected, and warnings when they are expected, generally 36 hours before. The timing is set by the arrival of tropical-storm-force winds, not of the centre, because preparations become difficult once these winds begin.

Lesson 9: Climate Change, Trends and Attribution
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Warming is expected to make the strongest storms stronger and wetter and their surges higher, but projected changes in total frequency are uncertain

The detection problem

Detecting trends in tropical-cyclone activity is difficult. Best-track records are inhomogeneous: aircraft reconnaissance began in the Atlantic in the 1940s, satellites in the 1960s, the Dvorak technique in the 1970s, and western Pacific reconnaissance ended in 1987. Short-lived storms in the open ocean were often missed before the satellite era, and intensity estimates of the strongest storms have changed with methods. Natural variability (ENSO, Atlantic multidecadal variability) is large relative to any anthropogenic signal, and aerosol forcing has influenced Atlantic activity independently of greenhouse gases. Reanalysed, homogenised satellite records such as that of Kossin et al. (2020) are therefore central to the trend question.

Observed changes

IPCC AR6 (2021) assessed that the proportion of major (Category 3–5) tropical cyclones has likely increased globally over the last four decades, and that the latitude at which western North Pacific storms reach peak intensity has shifted poleward. Kossin et al. (2020), using a homogenised satellite record for 1979–2017, found that the probability of a storm exceeding major-hurricane intensity increased by roughly 8 % per decade. Kossin, Emanuel and Vecchi (2014) documented a poleward migration of the location of lifetime maximum intensity in both hemispheres, consistent with tropical expansion. Some studies have also reported increases in the frequency of rapid intensification in the Atlantic. Attribution of these trends to human influence is assessed as more confident for some metrics than others, and the Atlantic increase since the 1970s is partly attributed to reduced aerosol pollution in addition to greenhouse warming.

Projected changes

The assessment by Knutson et al. (2020) for a 2 °C global warming scenario, consistent with IPCC AR6, concluded with medium-to-high confidence that: the global proportion of Category 4–5 storms will increase; average lifetime maximum intensity will increase by about 5 % (median); near-storm rain rates will increase by around 14 % (median); and storm surge levels will rise because of sea-level rise even if storms do not change. Most models project that the global number of tropical cyclones will decrease or remain unchanged, though a minority of approaches, notably some downscaling studies, project increases. Potential-intensity theory predicts an increase of a few per cent per kelvin of tropical warming, consistent with these modelling results.

Event attribution

Event attribution asks how human-caused warming changed the probability or magnitude of a specific event. Three Harvey studies published within months of the storm illustrate the approaches: Risser and Wehner (2017), using observed precipitation statistics, estimated that human influence increased Harvey's rainfall by at least 19 % with a best estimate of 38 %; van Oldenborgh et al. (2017), using observations and models, estimated an intensity increase of about 15 % (range 8–19 %) and a roughly threefold increase in probability; and Emanuel (2017) estimated that the annual probability of Harvey-like rainfall over Texas had increased from about 1 % in 1981–2000 to about 6 % by the late twenty-first century under high emissions. Storyline (pseudo-global-warming) approaches re-simulate the event with the warming signal removed. Attribution of tropical-cyclone wind intensity remains harder than attribution of rainfall.

Open questions

Major open questions include the controls on global tropical-cyclone frequency (why there are about 85 storms per year and not 30 or 300), the response of storm size and translation speed to warming, the contribution of aerosols to observed regional trends, and whether rapid intensification is becoming more common near coasts.

Lesson 10: Risk Reduction, Warnings and Evacuation
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability; most lives are saved by warning and evacuation, most property by building codes and land-use planning

The changing mortality record

Tropical-cyclone mortality has fallen dramatically in countries that invested in warning systems and shelters, despite rapid population growth in exposed areas. Bangladesh is the clearest example: the 1970 Bhola cyclone killed perhaps 300,000–500,000 people, the April 1991 cyclone about 138,000, while Cyclone Sidr (2007), a storm of comparable strength, killed about 3,400 according to official figures (some estimates are higher) and Cyclone Amphan (2020) fewer than 150 in Bangladesh and India combined. The improvement rests on the Cyclone Preparedness Programme, established in 1972 with the Bangladesh Red Crescent Society, its network of tens of thousands of trained volunteers, thousands of multi-purpose cyclone shelters, coastal embankments and mangrove belts, and improved forecasts from the India and Bangladesh meteorological departments. Cuba's civil-defence system is another widely cited success. Economic losses, by contrast, have risen steeply, driven mainly by increasing exposure: population and property values in hazard zones.

Evacuation planning

Evacuation is effective against surge, which is survivable only by leaving its footprint or by vertical evacuation into strong buildings; it is not necessary for most people outside surge zones, who are generally safer sheltering in well-built structures. The guiding principle is "run from water, hide from wind". Evacuation zones are defined from SLOSH envelopes (Lesson 6), and clearance times — the time needed to move the evacuating population to safety, from the start of the order until the last vehicle leaves — are calculated from road capacity, population, behaviour and shadow evacuation (people outside the zones who leave anyway). In large metropolitan areas clearance times can exceed 24–48 hours, which forces decisions while track and intensity forecasts are still uncertain. Contraflow (reversing inbound lanes) increases capacity. The Hurricane Rita evacuation of Houston in 2005, in which perhaps 2.5–3.7 million people took to the roads and heat and accidents during the evacuation caused more deaths than the storm itself, illustrates the cost of over-evacuation.

Behaviour and communication

Protective-action decisions depend on how warnings are received, understood and trusted. Social-science frameworks such as the Protective Action Decision Model of Lindell and Perry (2012) describe how people seek confirmation from social networks and environmental cues before acting. Barriers to evacuation include lack of transport, medical needs, pets, cost, prior false alarms and distrust of authorities; in Katrina, people without cars and those dependent on public services were disproportionately among the dead. Communication research has led to impact-based warnings, explicit surge inundation maps, and the move away from relying on Saffir–Simpson category alone.

Engineering and land use

Building codes, enforced well, are the most cost-effective defence against wind damage; their absence or poor enforcement was a principal lesson of Andrew. For surge, options range from elevation of buildings (for example the base-flood-elevation rules of the US National Flood Insurance Program), setbacks and managed retreat, to large engineered barriers such as the post-Katrina Hurricane and Storm Damage Risk Reduction System around New Orleans, the Inner Harbor Navigation Canal surge barrier and the proposed Galveston Bay barrier. Natural and nature-based defences — mangroves, wetlands, dunes and reefs — attenuate waves and, for broad wetland belts, can reduce surge, although their effect on large long-duration surges is limited.

Financial risk transfer

Insurance, reinsurance, catastrophe bonds and sovereign risk pools spread the financial losses. Hurricane Andrew drove eleven insurers into insolvency and led to the widespread use of catastrophe models and the creation of the Florida Hurricane Catastrophe Fund (1993) and, later, Citizens Property Insurance Corporation. The Caribbean Catastrophe Risk Insurance Facility, established in 2007 as the first multi-country parametric risk pool, makes rapid payouts to governments when modelled losses from wind, surge or rain exceed agreed thresholds. The Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction 2015–2030 provides the international policy context, emphasising understanding risk, governance, investment in resilience and preparedness for effective response.

Case Studies
═════════════════════════════════════════════

The following case studies span more than a century and all major basins. Each illustrates one or more of the hazards and lessons above. Death tolls for older and poorer-documented events are uncertain, sometimes by a factor of two, and losses are quoted in nominal dollars of the year unless stated otherwise.

1900 Galveston, Texas, United States

The Galveston hurricane of 8 September 1900 remains the deadliest natural disaster in US history. Galveston, a prosperous port on a barrier island whose highest point was about 2.7 m above sea level, had no seawall. The storm crossed Cuba as a tropical storm, intensified over the Gulf of Mexico and made landfall as a Category 4 hurricane with an estimated central pressure of about 936 hPa. A storm tide of about 4.5 m (15 ft) submerged the entire island; houses broke up and the debris, pushed by waves, acted as a battering ram against the buildings behind. Estimates of the death toll range from about 6,000 to 12,000, with 8,000 the most commonly quoted figure.

The disaster revealed the inadequacy of contemporary forecasting — the US Weather Bureau had dismissed Cuban forecasters' warnings and expected the storm to curve northward — and of coastal defences. Afterwards, Galveston built a concrete seawall (initial section completed 1904) and raised the grade of much of the city by up to about 5 m behind it, pumping sand from the harbour under the existing buildings. The seawall has since protected the city from several major storms, though Hurricane Ike in 2008 caused widespread flooding from the bay side, behind the wall.

1970 Bhola Cyclone, East Pakistan (Bangladesh)

The Bhola cyclone, which struck the coast of East Pakistan on the night of 12–13 November 1970, is the deadliest tropical cyclone on record. It was a strong storm, with maximum winds estimated at about 185 km/h, but its exceptional lethality came from a storm surge of several metres — estimates vary widely, and figures of up to about 10 m are often quoted — arriving at night near high tide over the low, densely populated islands and coastal lands of the Ganges–Brahmaputra–Meghna delta. The Bay of Bengal's funnel shape and broad shallow shelf favour very high surges. Estimates of the death toll range from 300,000 to 500,000 (Frank and Husain, 1971); the WMO lists it as the world's deadliest tropical cyclone.

Warnings existed but were not effectively communicated, there were few shelters, and many people did not understand the danger. The slow government response contributed to the political crisis that led to the independence of Bangladesh in 1971. In 1972 the Cyclone Preparedness Programme was launched, and over five decades of investment in shelters, volunteers and embankments has reduced cyclone mortality by more than a hundredfold.

1992 Hurricane Andrew, Bahamas and South Florida

Andrew was a compact, intense hurricane that crossed the northern Bahamas and struck southern Miami-Dade County near Homestead on 24 August 1992. It was originally assessed as Category 4, but a reanalysis published in 2004 (Landsea et al.) raised its landfall intensity to 145 kt (165 mph) and Category 5, with a landfall pressure of about 922 hPa. Its radius of maximum wind was only about 19 km, so the swath of extreme winds was narrow. Andrew caused direct damage of about US$27 billion in 1992 dollars, then the costliest natural disaster in US history, and 26 direct deaths in the United States and the Bahamas, with dozens more indirect deaths.

The damage was disproportionate to the hazard because of poor construction and lax code enforcement: many houses lost roofs because of weak sheathing and roof-to-wall connections, and wind-borne debris breached windows. Andrew led to the Miami-Dade product approval system, the statewide Florida Building Code of 2002, the wide adoption of catastrophe modelling and major restructuring of the Florida insurance market. It also became a standard case for the study of eyewall mesovortices and the reduction of flight-level winds to the surface.

1998 Hurricane Mitch, Central America

Mitch reached Category 5 in the western Caribbean on 26 October 1998, with a minimum central pressure of 905 hPa, one of the lowest on record for an October Atlantic hurricane. It weakened substantially before making landfall in Honduras, but then moved very slowly across Honduras and Nicaragua for several days, producing extraordinary rainfall: the highest gauge totals were about 900 mm, and satellite-based estimates suggest up to about 1,900 mm (75 in.) in some mountainous areas. Floods and landslides killed at least 11,000 people, with many thousands more missing; the total is often given as 11,000–19,000. In Nicaragua, a flank collapse on the Casita volcano generated a lahar that buried the towns of El Porvenir and Rolando Rodríguez near Posoltega, killing more than 2,000 people.

Mitch is the deadliest Atlantic hurricane since the Great Hurricane of 1780. It demonstrated that rainfall rather than wind can dominate the hazard, that slow motion and orography are decisive, and that vulnerability — deforested slopes, informal settlement on floodplains and weak infrastructure — can set back development by decades; Honduras's president estimated that the storm had erased fifty years of progress.

2005 Hurricane Katrina, Gulf Coast, United States

Katrina reached Category 5 over the Gulf of Mexico on 28 August 2005, with a minimum pressure of 902 hPa, after crossing the warm Loop Current. It weakened to Category 3 before landfall in south-eastern Louisiana on 29 August (about 110 kt, 920 hPa) and a final landfall on the Louisiana–Mississippi border. Because the storm had been very large and intense shortly before landfall, its surge reflected its earlier intensity and size more than its landfall category: the Mississippi coast experienced a storm tide of about 7–8.5 m, the highest observed in US history.

In New Orleans, surge overtopped levees and floodwalls and caused several catastrophic failures, including foundation failures of I-walls on the 17th Street and London Avenue drainage canals at water levels below their design heights. About 80 % of the city flooded. The Interagency Performance Evaluation Task Force (IPET) and independent reviews by the American Society of Civil Engineers concluded that the hurricane protection system was a "system in name only", with design flaws, inconsistent standards and incomplete construction. The death toll is commonly given as between about 1,400 and 1,833, depending on the source and the treatment of indirect deaths; damage was about US$125 billion (2005 dollars). The failures led to a US$14.5 billion reconstruction of the New Orleans defences and to changes in US emergency management.

2008 Cyclone Nargis, Myanmar

Nargis made landfall in the Irrawaddy (Ayeyarwady) delta of Myanmar on 2 May 2008, with winds equivalent to a Category 3–4 hurricane. Its track paralleled the low-lying delta coast, and a surge of about 3–5 m penetrated tens of kilometres inland over flat, densely populated rice land with few elevated refuges. According to the official tally, about 84,500 people were killed and 53,800 went missing, giving an estimated toll of around 138,000. The unusual track, the absence of an effective warning and evacuation system, and the destruction of mangroves along the coast in previous decades all contributed. The military government initially restricted international aid. Nargis became a case study in the importance of evacuation infrastructure in densely populated deltas, and in the sensitivity of surge to track direction relative to the coast.

2013 Typhoon Haiyan (Yolanda), Philippines

Haiyan made landfall in Eastern Samar on 8 November 2013 at or near peak intensity, with 1-minute winds estimated by JTWC at 170 kt (about 315 km/h) and 10-minute winds estimated by JMA at 125 kt (230 km/h) with a central pressure of 895 hPa; it is among the most intense tropical cyclones ever to make landfall, although the intensity estimates are satellite-based and uncertain. A storm surge of 5–7 m flooded Tacloban City at the head of Leyte Gulf, a funnel-shaped embayment that amplified the surge, and a bore generated over a fringing reef struck Hernani. The Philippine government reported about 6,300 deaths, with more than 1,000 missing.

Warnings were issued days in advance, and many people evacuated, but the phrase "storm surge" was not widely understood, and some shelters were themselves inundated. Haiyan prompted the Philippines to adopt clearer surge terminology and impact-based warnings, and became a reference case for surge on reef-fronted coasts.

2017 Hurricanes Harvey and Maria, Texas and Puerto Rico

Harvey rapidly intensified over the western Gulf of Mexico and made landfall near Rockport, Texas, late on 25 August 2017 (local time) as a Category 4 hurricane (about 115 kt). Weak steering currents caused it to stall over south-eastern Texas for several days, producing the highest storm-total rainfall recorded for a US tropical cyclone: 1,539 mm (60.58 in.) near Nederland, Texas. Catastrophic flooding in the Houston metropolitan area and surrounding regions caused about 68 direct deaths and damage of about US$125 billion. Rapid attribution studies (Lesson 9) concluded that human-caused warming had substantially increased Harvey's rainfall.

Less than a month later, Hurricane Maria devastated Dominica as a Category 5 hurricane and made landfall in Puerto Rico on 20 September 2017 as a high-end Category 4 (about 135 kt). Wind and rain destroyed most of the island's electrical grid, causing the longest blackout in US history, which lasted months for many residents, and disrupted water, communications and medical care. Mortality has been contentious: the initial official count was 64, but excess-mortality studies estimated far more. A Harvard study (Kishore et al., 2018) estimated about 4,600 excess deaths with a wide confidence interval (roughly 800–8,500), and a study commissioned by the government from George Washington University estimated 2,975 excess deaths from September 2017 through February 2018, which was adopted as the official figure. Maria showed that indirect deaths from the collapse of infrastructure can exceed direct deaths by more than an order of magnitude, and that mortality surveillance in disasters needs to capture them.

2022 Hurricane Ian, Florida, United States

Ian underwent rapid intensification over the eastern Gulf of Mexico and reached Category 5 intensity (about 140 kt in post-storm analysis) shortly before making landfall on the south-western Florida coast near Cayo Costa on 28 September 2022 as a high-end Category 4 (about 130 kt). Forecast tracks in the days before landfall had pointed further north, towards Tampa Bay, and the shift south reduced the effective warning time for Lee County, where evacuation orders were issued later than in neighbouring counties. A storm surge of about 3–4.5 m devastated Fort Myers Beach, Sanibel Island and neighbouring communities. Ian caused about 150 deaths in Florida, including 66 direct deaths, most of which were drownings in the storm surge (the rest were indirect deaths, for example during clean-up or from interrupted medical care), making Ian the deadliest hurricane in Florida since 1935. Damage was estimated at about US$110–115 billion, among the three costliest US hurricanes. Ian renewed debate about evacuation timing under track uncertainty, the communication of surge risk to coastal residents and visitors, and the affordability of insurance in Florida.
