The Wind Hazard and Structural Engineering

Lesson 5 of 10

Overview12345678910Case studiesReferences

Key idea of this section

Wind pressure q = ½ρV² grows with the square of wind speed, and observed damage rises even faster — roughly with the cube or higher power of wind speed

From wind speed to load

The dynamic pressure exerted by wind of speed V on a surface normal to the flow is q = ½ρV². With ρ = 1.225 kg/m³, a wind of 50 m/s exerts about 1.5 kPa and one of 70 m/s about 3.0 kPa: a 40 % increase in wind speed nearly doubles the load. Real buildings experience pressures that are this reference value multiplied by pressure coefficients that depend on geometry, and the most damaging loads are suction (negative pressure) on roofs, at roof corners and edges and on leeward walls. Building codes such as ASCE 7 in the United States express the design velocity pressure as qz = 0.613 Kz Kzt Kd V² (in N/m² with V in m/s), where Kz accounts for height and terrain exposure, Kzt for topographic speed-up and Kd for wind directionality. Design wind speeds are mapped as 3-second gusts at 10 m in open terrain, for specified return periods (for ordinary buildings in ASCE 7-16 and 7-22, a mean recurrence interval of 700 years).

Failure modes

Post-storm damage surveys after Hurricane Andrew (1992) and subsequent storms identified a consistent sequence of failures in light-frame construction: loss of roof covering and sheathing, especially at gable ends and corners; breach of windows and doors by wind-borne debris, which pressurises the interior and adds to the uplift on the roof; failure of the roof-to-wall connection; and, in the worst cases, collapse of walls. Engineered responses include hurricane straps and clips that create a continuous load path from roof to foundation, closer nailing of roof sheathing, sealed roof decks, impact-resistant glazing or shutters tested against standard missile impacts, and hip rather than gable roofs. The Florida Building Code, adopted statewide in 2002 largely as a result of Andrew, made many of these provisions mandatory; damage studies after Hurricane Charley (2004) and later storms found substantially lower losses in houses built to the post-2002 code.

Boundary-layer and terrain effects

Surface winds over land are reduced relative to winds over water by increased roughness, but gusts become relatively stronger. Topography produces speed-up over hills and escarpments, and the boundary layer of intense storms contains small-scale features — boundary-layer rolls and eyewall mesovortices — that can produce localised streaks of extreme damage. Tornadoes are common in the outer rainbands of landfalling storms, especially in the right-front quadrant; Hurricane Ivan (2004) produced more than 100 tornadoes in the United States.

Wind-field models and damage functions

Catastrophe models used by insurers and reinsurers simulate many thousands of synthetic storms, compute the wind field of each with parametric profiles such as Holland's (Lesson 4) and a boundary-layer model, apply terrain and roughness corrections, and translate local peak gusts into damage ratios using vulnerability (fragility) curves calibrated on claims data. Empirical studies of aggregated losses suggest that damage scales with wind speed to a power of three or higher; Emanuel's power dissipation index (Emanuel, 2005), PDI = ∫ Vmax³ dt, was introduced as a climate metric for this reason. The kinetic energy dissipated by surface friction in a single mature storm, of order ρ CD V³ integrated over the core area, is about 3 × 10¹² W for typical values.

Exercise

  • (a)
    Compute the reference dynamic pressure ½ρV² for winds of 50 m/s and 70 m/s (ρ = 1.225 kg/m³). By what factor does the load increase, and by what factor would damage increase if it scales with V³?
  • (b)
    Estimate the frictional dissipation rate D ≈ π ρ CD V³ R² for V = 50 m/s, R = 60 km, ρ = 1.2 kg/m³ and CD = 2 × 10⁻³.
Show solution

Answers: (a) q(50) = 0.5 × 1.225 × 2,500 ≈ 1,531 Pa; q(70) = 0.5 × 1.225 × 4,900 ≈ 3,001 Pa. The load ratio is (70/50)² = 1.96, the cubic ratio (70/50)³ ≈ 2.74. (b) D ≈ π × 1.2 × 0.002 × 125,000 × 3.6 × 10⁹ ≈ 3.4 × 10¹² W, a few terawatts, which is of the same order as Emanuel's estimate and small compared with the latent-heat release of order 10¹⁴ W.
