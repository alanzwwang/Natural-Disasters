Rainfall and Inland Flooding

Lesson 7 of 10

Overview12345678910Case studiesReferences

Key idea of this section

Storm total rainfall ≈ (mean rain rate) × (rain-area diameter) / (translation speed)

Rain production

A tropical cyclone is an efficient converter of water vapour to precipitation. Rain rates in the eyewall commonly exceed 50 mm/h and storm-average rates within 100–200 km of the centre are of order 5–15 mm/h. The total rainfall at a point depends far more on how long the rain lasts there than on how intense the storm is: slow-moving and stalled storms produce the greatest totals, regardless of their wind category. Tropical Storm Allison (2001) flooded Houston without ever becoming a hurricane, and Tropical Storm Claudette (1979) dropped about 1,070 mm (42 in.) in 24 hours at Alvin, Texas. Several world-record point rainfalls, including those for 12 hours, 24 hours and 72 hours, were produced by tropical cyclones on La Réunion, where steep orography amplifies rain from slow-moving storms; Cyclone Denise (1966) produced about 1,825 mm in 24 hours at Foc-Foc.

Factors controlling rainfall

Four factors combine. Storm motion sets duration; storm size sets the width of the rain swath; environmental moisture (total precipitable water, typically 50–70 mm in the tropics) and storm intensity set the rain rate; and topography enhances rain on windward slopes and can lift moist air in otherwise weak systems, as in Hurricane Mitch (1998) over Honduras and Nicaragua and Typhoon Morakot (2009) over Taiwan, where more than 2,700 mm fell in about four days in the mountains. Interaction with fronts during extratropical transition can produce heavy rain far from the original circulation, as with Hurricane Floyd (1999) in North Carolina. Simple climatology-and-persistence rainfall models (R-CLIPER) capture the first-order dependence on motion and intensity; dynamical models now provide most operational guidance.

The Clausius–Clapeyron constraint

Saturation vapour pressure rises by about 7 % per kelvin of warming at tropical temperatures (the Clausius–Clapeyron relation). Because tropical cyclones converge moisture from a large area, their rain rates are expected to scale at roughly this rate or somewhat faster; model studies project increases in near-core rain rates of the order of 10–15 % for 2 °C of global warming (Knutson et al., 2020). Attribution studies of Hurricane Harvey (Lesson 9) found rainfall totals substantially larger than the Clausius–Clapeyron rate alone would suggest, though with wide uncertainty ranges.

Inland flooding and mortality

Freshwater flooding has become a leading cause of tropical-cyclone deaths in the United States. Rappaport (2014) found that rain-induced floods caused about a quarter of direct US deaths over 1963–2012, many of them in vehicles driven into flooded roads, and the proportion was much higher in years without major surge events. In Central America, the Caribbean and parts of Asia, rainfall-triggered flash floods, landslides and debris flows account for most deaths, as in Mitch (1998), Hurricane Jeanne in Haiti (2004, about 3,000 deaths, mostly in Gonaïves) and Hurricane Stan (2005) in Guatemala. Compound flooding — the simultaneous occurrence of surge and river or pluvial flooding — is a particular hazard in estuaries and urban drainage systems.

Translation speed trends

Kossin (2018) reported a global decrease of about 10 % in tropical-cyclone translation speed over 1949–2016, which would increase local rainfall totals, but subsequent studies (for example Moon, Kim and Chan, 2019, and Lanzante, 2019) questioned how much of the trend reflects changes in observing practice and natural variability. The trend in stalling over the US coastline remains an active research question.

Exercise

  • (a)
    A storm with an average rain rate of 10 mm/h over a rain area 300 km in diameter passes over a point at 18 km/h. Estimate the storm-total rainfall. Repeat for a stalled storm drifting at 1 m/s (3.6 km/h).
  • (b)
    If rain rates scale at 7 % per kelvin, by what percentage would they increase for 1.5 K of warming?
Show solution

Answers: (a) Duration = 300 km / 18 km/h ≈ 16.7 h, so total ≈ 10 mm/h × 16.7 h ≈ 170 mm. At 3.6 km/h, duration ≈ 83 h and total ≈ 830 mm — the same storm produces five times more rain simply by moving five times more slowly. (b) 1.071.5 ≈ 1.107, an increase of about 11 % (or about 10.5 % if the linear approximation 7 × 1.5 is used).
