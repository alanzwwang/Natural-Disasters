The Hurricane as a Heat Engine and Potential Intensity

Lesson 2 of 10

Overview12345678910Case studiesReferences

Key idea of this section

Vmax² ≈ (Ck/CD) · [(Ts − To)/To] · (k₀* − k)

The energy source

The energy of a tropical cyclone comes from the transfer of enthalpy — overwhelmingly latent heat in the form of water vapour, with a smaller sensible-heat contribution — from the ocean to the atmosphere. The tropical boundary layer is normally undersaturated with respect to the sea surface: air in the boundary layer is cooler and drier than the saturated air in contact with the water. In the high winds of a hurricane core this disequilibrium drives large surface fluxes. The bulk aerodynamic formula for the enthalpy flux is

Fk = ρ Ck |V| (k₀* − k)

where ρ is air density, Ck the enthalpy exchange coefficient, |V| the near-surface wind speed, k₀* the saturation specific enthalpy of air at the sea-surface temperature and pressure, and k the specific enthalpy of the boundary-layer air. Because the flux is proportional to wind speed, stronger winds draw more energy from the ocean — a positive feedback, termed wind-induced surface heat exchange (WISHE), that underpins the air–sea interaction theory of Emanuel (1986). The total rate of latent-heat release is enormous: a storm raining an average of 1.5 cm per day over a circle of radius 665 km releases about 6 × 10¹⁴ W, the figure Emanuel uses to illustrate that a hurricane's heat release is of order a hundred times humanity's total electrical generating capacity. Only a few per cent of this heat is converted to kinetic energy.

The Carnot cycle

Emanuel's idealised mature hurricane operates as a Carnot cycle with four legs. (1) Air spirals inward in the boundary layer at nearly constant temperature Ts, gaining enthalpy isothermally from the sea. (2) It ascends adiabatically in the eyewall, conserving moist entropy, to the outflow level. (3) It flows out at the tropopause and loses heat by infrared radiation at a nearly constant low temperature To (approximately 190–210 K). (4) It descends slowly far from the centre, compressing and losing entropy radiatively, to close the cycle. The thermodynamic efficiency of a Carnot engine is

ε = (Ts − To) / Ts

and for Ts = 300 K and To = 200 K, ε ≈ 1/3. The mechanical energy generated is dissipated mostly by surface friction in the boundary layer near the radius of maximum winds. Bister and Emanuel (1998) showed that this frictional dissipation produces heat in the boundary layer that is itself recycled into the engine, which raises the effective efficiency so that To rather than Ts appears in the denominator.

Potential intensity

Balancing the rate of energy generation against the rate of frictional dissipation (proportional to ρ CD |V|³) at the radius of maximum wind gives the potential intensity:

Vp² = (Ck/CD) · [(Ts − To) / To] · (k₀* − k)

Vp is the theoretical maximum gradient wind speed near the top of the boundary layer for a steady storm in a given environment. Over the tropical oceans in late summer, computed potential intensity is typically 60–85 m/s, and the theory reproduces the observed upper bound of storm intensity reasonably well: very few storms exceed their local potential intensity, although the strongest (Patricia in 2015, with about 185 kt or 95 m/s estimated) approach or locally exceed some estimates. Most storms never reach their potential intensity: the distribution of lifetime maximum intensity normalised by potential intensity is broad, with the median storm reaching roughly half of it, because shear, dry air, ocean cooling, land interaction and limited lifetime all intervene.

The exchange coefficients

The ratio Ck/CD matters as much as the thermodynamics. Measurements at high wind speeds are extremely difficult, but GPS dropwindsonde profiles (Powell, Vickery and Reinhold, 2003) showed that the drag coefficient CD stops increasing with wind speed above about 30–35 m/s and levels off at roughly 2–2.5 × 10⁻³, plausibly because sea spray and a "slip" layer of foam alter the momentum transfer. Field and laboratory work, including the CBLAST campaign (2003–2004), suggests that Ck remains roughly constant near 1.0–1.2 × 10⁻³, giving Ck/CD of order 0.5–1. Emanuel's theory requires Ck/CD of roughly 0.75 or more to explain observed intense storms, and the role of sea spray in enhancing enthalpy flux remains an open research question.

Ocean coupling

A hurricane cools the sea beneath it. Wind stress drives strong inertial currents in the ocean mixed layer, and the associated shear-driven turbulence entrains colder water from the thermocline; upwelling under the storm core adds to the effect. The resulting cold wake can be 1–6 °C cooler than the surrounding sea, and the cooling under a slow-moving storm can reduce its intensity substantially. For this reason the relevant thermodynamic quantity is often taken to be the tropical cyclone heat potential or ocean heat content (the integrated heat above the 26 °C isotherm, in kJ/cm²), or the temperature averaged over the upper 80–100 m, rather than the skin sea-surface temperature. Warm-core eddies shed by the Gulf of Mexico Loop Current, with deep warm layers, have been implicated in the rapid intensification of several storms, including Opal (1995), Katrina and Rita (2005).

Exercise

  • (a)
    Compute the Carnot efficiency (Ts − To)/Ts and the Bister–Emanuel factor (Ts − To)/To for Ts = 301 K and To = 200 K.
  • (b)
    Using the potential-intensity formula with Ck/CD = 0.9 and a disequilibrium k₀* − k = 12 kJ/kg, compute Vp. Express it in knots and identify its Saffir–Simpson category.
Show solution

Answers: (a) Carnot efficiency = 101/301 ≈ 0.336; Bister–Emanuel factor = 101/200 = 0.505. (b) Vp² = 0.9 × 0.505 × 12,000 J/kg ≈ 5,454 m²/s², so Vp ≈ 73.9 m/s. Dividing by 0.5144 m/s per knot gives ≈ 144 kt, which is Category 5 (≥ 137 kt). Note that this is a gradient-level wind; surface winds would be somewhat lower.
