Earthquake Generation and Seafloor Deformation

Megathrust earthquakes

Roughly four-fifths of damaging tsunamis recorded historically were generated by earthquakes, and nearly all great earthquake tsunamis originate on subduction-zone megathrusts, the shallow-dipping plate interfaces where oceanic lithosphere descends beneath an overriding plate. Between earthquakes, friction on the locked interface drags the leading edge of the overriding plate down and back; during rupture, the stored elastic strain is released and the seaward part of the overriding plate rebounds upward while the region farther landward subsides. Plafker's field mapping after the 1964 Alaska earthquake documented uplift of up to about 11.5 m on Montague Island and subsidence of about 2 m around the Kenai Peninsula, providing one of the clearest demonstrations of this elastic-rebound pattern.

The size of an earthquake is quantified by the seismic moment M0 = μAD, where μ is the rigidity of the rock near the fault (typically 30–50 GPa for subduction interfaces), A the rupture area and D the average slip. Moment magnitude follows from the Hanks–Kanamori relation, written in SI units as Mw = (2/3)(log₁₀ M0 − 9.1). Each unit increase in Mw corresponds to about a 32-fold increase in moment. A great megathrust earthquake of Mw 9 typically ruptures a fault 400–1,000 km long and 100–200 km wide with average slips of 10–30 m.

From fault slip to seafloor uplift

The static displacement of the seafloor caused by slip on a rectangular dislocation in an elastic half-space is given by closed-form expressions published by Okada (1985), which remain the standard way to convert finite-fault models into tsunami initial conditions. The vertical uplift pattern depends on dip, depth, slip and rake: a shallow thrust produces a narrow, high uplift lobe above the up-dip edge of slip and a broader, lower subsidence trough landward. Horizontal seafloor motion also contributes where the seabed slopes steeply, as on the inner trench wall; Tanioka and Satake (1996) showed that the term uh · ∇h, the horizontal seafloor displacement multiplied by the bathymetric gradient, can add significantly to effective uplift for shallow ruptures near trenches.

Because rupture velocities of 2–3 km/s are far faster than tsunami speeds, most earthquake tsunamis can be modelled with an instantaneous initial condition: the sea surface is assumed to copy the seafloor uplift at t = 0, with zero initial velocity. This fails for very slow ruptures and for long faults like the 2004 Sumatra–Andaman rupture, where slip took about 8–10 minutes to propagate roughly 1,300 km northward and a time-dependent (kinematic) source improves fits to observations.

The Kajiura filter

The sea surface does not copy small-scale seafloor features exactly. Kajiura (1963) showed that a seafloor displacement with horizontal wavenumber k transmits to the surface attenuated by a factor 1/cosh(kh). Features with a horizontal scale comparable to or smaller than the water depth are smoothed out, while features tens of times wider than the depth are transmitted almost unchanged.

ηsurface(k) = ηseafloor(k) / cosh(kh)

For a seafloor bump with a wavelength of twice the water depth (kh = π), only 1/cosh(π) ≈ 9 % of the amplitude reaches the surface; for a wavelength ten times the depth (kh ≈ 0.63), about 83 % does. Modern practice applies this filter to Okada displacements, especially for deep-water sources and for the short-wavelength uplift associated with splay faults.

Tsunami energy

The potential energy of the initial displacement, relative to a flat sea, is E = ½ ρ g ∫ η² dA; for a propagating linear wave the energy is shared equally between potential and kinetic forms. Only a small fraction of seismic energy, typically of order 1 % or less, ends up in the tsunami, but because E scales with the square of uplift and with area, a great megathrust earthquake produces tsunami energies of order 1014–1016 J (estimates for 2004 and 2011 are a few times 1015 J). Rupture depth and the share of slip near the trench matter enormously: two earthquakes of identical Mw can produce tsunamis differing by an order of magnitude in amplitude depending on how much slip reaches the shallow, compliant part of the interface.

Directivity

A long rupture radiates tsunami energy preferentially perpendicular to its strike, much as a long antenna beams radio waves. The initial wavefront is roughly an elongated ridge, and the waves that leave it broadside remain coherent and high, whereas those travelling along strike spread more quickly. This is why the 2004 tsunami was devastating in Sri Lanka, India and Thailand, perpendicular to the north–south rupture, but weak at comparable distances to the north along the rupture axis, and why the 2011 Tōhoku tsunami beamed energy south-eastward across the Pacific.

Exercise

  • (a)
    A megathrust rupture is 400 km long and 150 km wide with an average slip of 20 m on a fault with rigidity 40 GPa. Compute M0 and Mw.
  • (b)
    Approximate the initial sea-surface uplift as a uniform 2 m over a 200 km × 100 km area. Estimate the potential energy of the tsunami.
Show solution

Answers: (a) M0 = 4.0 × 1010 Pa × (4.0 × 105 m × 1.5 × 105 m) × 20 m = 4.8 × 1022 N·m. Then Mw = (2/3)(log₁₀ 4.8 × 1022 − 9.1) = (2/3)(22.68 − 9.1) ≈ 9.05, a great earthquake comparable to Tōhoku 2011. (b) E = ½ × 1,025 × 9.81 × (2)² × (2.0 × 105 × 1.0 × 105) ≈ 4.0 × 1014 J, roughly the energy of 100 kilotonnes of TNT (1 kt ≈ 4.18 × 1012 J). A real uplift pattern is non-uniform, which concentrates energy because E depends on η².
