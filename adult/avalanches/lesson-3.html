Slabs, Weak Layers and Snowpack Stress

Lesson 3 of 10

Overview12345678910Case studiesReferences

Key idea of this section

A dry slab avalanche needs a slab, a weak layer, a steep enough bed and a trigger: τ = ρ g D sin ψ, σn = ρ g D cos ψ

The ingredients of a slab avalanche

Dry slab avalanches cause the great majority of avalanche fatalities. They require a cohesive slab (a layer or set of layers able to store elastic energy and transmit tension), a weak layer or interface beneath it, a bed surface, and a slope steep enough that the slab slides once its basal support is lost. Slab thickness is commonly 0.2–1 m for skier-triggered avalanches, but natural and deep persistent slabs can exceed 2–3 m. The weak layer is typically a few millimetres to a few centimetres thick: buried surface hoar, faceted crystals, depth hoar, facets on a crust, or occasionally poorly bonded new snow such as graupel.

Stresses on an inclined snowpack

For a uniform slab of density ρ and slope-normal thickness D on a slope inclined at ψ, the gravitational stress on the weak layer resolves into a normal stress and a slope-parallel shear stress:

σn = ρ g D cos ψ
τ = ρ g D sin ψ

If depth is measured vertically (as with a ruler pushed vertically into the snow, Hv), then D = Hv cos ψ. Snow depth on a slope must always be specified as slope-normal or vertical, since the two differ by 20–30% on typical avalanche slopes. Field measurements with shear frames show that weak-layer shear strengths range from a few hundred pascals to a few kilopascals; buried surface hoar can be among the weakest layers, and strength increases with time and overburden as the layer sinters.

Stability indices

A natural stability index is the ratio of shear strength to applied shear stress, S = τp / τ. Natural release becomes likely as S approaches 1. For skier triggering, Föhn (1987) defined a skier stability index S' = τp / (τ + Δτ), where Δτ is the additional shear stress caused by a skier, computed from elastic solutions for a line load on an inclined half-space. Because the added stress decays roughly inversely with depth, skiers rarely initiate failures in weak layers deeper than about 1 m, which is why skier-triggered slabs are mostly 0.3–1 m thick. Schweizer and Jamieson (2001) showed that skier-triggered slabs are associated with soft slabs over thin persistent weak layers, large differences in hardness and grain size across the failure interface, and shallow failure depths. Practitioners have summarised such properties as "yellow flags" or "lemons": structural variables such as a large hardness difference, large grain-size difference, persistent grain type and failure depth less than 1 m. Stability indices based on strength alone are, however, incomplete: whether a local failure grows into an avalanche depends on crack propagation, the subject of Lesson 4.

Avalanche problems

Modern forecasting describes hazard in terms of typical avalanche problems. The European Avalanche Warning Services (EAWS) recognise five: new snow, wind-drifted snow, persistent weak layers, wet snow and gliding snow. The North American Conceptual Model of Avalanche Hazard (Statham et al., 2018) uses nine: dry loose, wet loose, storm slab, wind slab, persistent slab, deep persistent slab, wet slab, glide avalanche and cornice fall. Each problem has characteristic locations, sizes, likelihoods of triggering and time scales; for example, wind slabs form on lee slopes and typically stabilise within days, whereas deep persistent slabs are difficult to trigger but can produce very large avalanches weeks after the weak layer was buried.

Spatial variability

The snowpack varies across a slope on scales from centimetres to hundreds of metres, as wind deposition, solar input, vegetation and ground roughness alter layer thickness and grain type. Spatial variability means that a single pit result does not represent a slope: a slope with a widespread persistent weak layer may contain trigger points where the slab is thin and the skier stress reaches the weak layer. Whether variability promotes or hinders release depends on its scale relative to the critical crack length; this remains an active research subject.

Exercise

  • (a)
    A slab of density 200 kg m⁻³ and slope-normal thickness 0.50 m lies on a 38° slope. Compute the normal and shear stresses on the weak layer.
  • (b)
    Shear-frame tests give a weak-layer shear strength of 900 Pa. Compute the natural stability index, and comment.
  • (c)
    A vertical depth measurement of 1.5 m was made on the same slope. What is the slope-normal thickness?
Show solution

Answers: (a) ρ g D = 200 × 9.81 × 0.50 = 981 Pa; σn = 981 × cos 38° ≈ 773 Pa and τ = 981 × sin 38° ≈ 604 Pa. (b) S = 900 / 604 ≈ 1.49. The slope is unlikely to release naturally, but a value below about 1.5 is generally regarded as poor; the added stress from a skier near the surface of a 0.5 m slab could locally exceed the strength. (c) D = 1.5 × cos 38° ≈ 1.18 m.
