Effective Stress and Shear Strength

Lesson 2 of 10

Overview12345678910Case studiesReferences

Key idea of this section

τf = c′ + (σ − u) tan φ′ = c′ + σ′ tan φ′

Terzaghi's principle

The behaviour of soil depends not on total stress but on effective stress, the stress carried by the grain skeleton. Terzaghi's principle states that for a saturated soil the effective normal stress σ′ equals the total normal stress σ minus the pore-water pressure u. Pore pressure acts equally in all directions, carries no shear, and pushes grains apart; raising u at constant σ reduces the frictional resistance between grains. This single relation explains why most landslides follow rainfall, snowmelt, rapid drawdown of a reservoir or earthquake shaking: each of these increases pore pressure on a potential surface of rupture.

In hydrostatic conditions below a water table the pore pressure at depth hw below the water table is u = γw hw, where γw ≈ 9.81 kN/m³. Under seepage, u depends on the flow net; for slope-parallel seepage it is reduced by the factor cos²β, as derived in Lesson 3. Artesian conditions, where u exceeds hydrostatic, are common beneath low-permeability layers, for example in layered glacial sediments and in many coastal bluffs; elevated groundwater pressures in such deposits are thought to have contributed to the Oso failure in 2014.

The Mohr–Coulomb criterion

Shear strength on a plane is expressed by the Mohr–Coulomb criterion in effective-stress terms, τf = c′ + σ′ tan φ′, where c′ is the effective cohesion and φ′ the effective angle of internal friction. For clean sands and gravels c′ ≈ 0 and φ′ ≈ 30–40°; for normally consolidated clays c′ is small and φ′ ≈ 20–30°; for overconsolidated clays at peak strength c′ may be tens of kilopascals. Apparent cohesion in unsaturated soils arises from matric suction (ua − uw); Fredlund's extended criterion adds a term (ua − uw) tan φb. When rain infiltrates, suction is lost and the apparent cohesion that held a steep cut or a thin colluvial mantle can vanish without any positive pore pressure developing. Root reinforcement provides a further cohesion-like term, typically 1–20 kPa in forest soils, which declines over several years after logging or wildfire as roots decay.

Peak, critical-state and residual strength

Dense soils dilate when sheared and show a peak strength above their critical-state strength; loose soils contract, and when saturated and sheared rapidly they generate positive excess pore pressure. This contractive behaviour underlies static liquefaction and flowslides: if a loose, saturated mass begins to deform, pore pressure rises, effective stress falls and strength collapses, so the mass accelerates. Iverson et al. (2000) demonstrated in large flume experiments that identical sandy soils behaved completely differently depending on initial porosity: loose soil failed abruptly and mobilised as a debris flow, whereas dense soil crept episodically because dilation reduced pore pressure.

In clays, large displacements align platy minerals parallel to the shear surface and reduce strength to the residual value φ′r, which may be as low as 5–15° in smectite-rich clays. First-time slides should be analysed with peak or fully softened strength, but reactivated slides with residual strength. The Vajont failure surface lay in thin clay interbeds with low residual friction angles — of order 10° in many laboratory studies — although the values and their role in the failure remain debated.

Rock masses and discontinuities

In rock, failure is usually controlled by discontinuities — bedding, joints, faults, foliation. Shear strength of a rough joint is often described by Barton's relation τ = σ′ tan[φb + JRC log10(JCS/σ′)], where φb is the basic friction angle, JRC the joint roughness coefficient and JCS the joint wall compressive strength. Strength of the rock mass as a whole is commonly described with the non-linear Hoek–Brown criterion, parameterised by the Geological Strength Index. Progressive failure — the gradual breaking of intact rock bridges and the reduction of strength from peak to residual along a developing surface — explains why large rock slopes may deform for decades before catastrophic collapse.

Stress paths to failure

Field slopes fail along a variety of stress paths. Rainfall usually raises pore pressure at nearly constant total stress, moving the Mohr circle leftward toward the failure envelope. Undercutting by a river or excavation reduces confining stress. Loading of the head by fill or a spoil tip increases driving shear stress. Earthquakes add transient inertial stresses and may generate excess pore pressure in loose saturated soils. Distinguishing these paths matters, because drained and undrained responses can give very different strengths for the same soil.

Exercise

  • (a)
    A silty sand has a unit weight of 18 kN/m³ above the water table and 20 kN/m³ below. The water table is 1 m below a level ground surface. Compute the total stress, pore pressure and vertical effective stress at 4 m depth (hydrostatic conditions).
  • (b)
    With c′ = 8 kPa and φ′ = 30°, what is the shear strength on a horizontal plane at that depth?
Show solution

Answers: (a) σ = 18 × 1 + 20 × 3 = 78 kPa; u = 9.81 × 3 = 29.4 kPa; σ′ = 78 − 29.4 = 48.6 kPa. (b) τf = 8 + 48.6 × tan 30° = 8 + 48.6 × 0.577 ≈ 36.0 kPa.
