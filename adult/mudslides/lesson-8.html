Landslide Dams and Outburst Floods

Formation of landslide dams

When a landslide or debris flow enters a narrow valley it can block the river and impound a lake. Costa and Schuster (1988), reviewing several hundred cases, found that rock and debris avalanches, slumps and slides, and debris flows are the commonest dam-forming types, with rainfall and earthquakes the commonest triggers. Dams are most likely where landslide volume is large relative to the valley cross-section and river discharge is too small to remove material as fast as it arrives. Earthquakes are particularly effective because they trigger many large landslides at once: the 2008 Wenchuan earthquake created more than 250 landslide dams, and the 1911 Sarez earthquake in Tajikistan built the Usoi dam, roughly 2 km³ of rock and more than 500 m high, which still impounds Lake Sarez, about 17 km³ of water, posing a long-recognised regional hazard.

Longevity

Most landslide dams are short-lived. In Costa and Schuster's compilation of dams that failed, roughly a quarter failed within a day of formation, about half within ten days and the great majority within a year. Failure is almost always by overtopping and headward erosion of the crest, because dams are composed of poorly sorted, uncompacted material and have no engineered spillway; seepage erosion (piping) and slope failure of the downstream face are less common. Stability can be screened with geomorphic indices such as the Dimensionless Blockage Index of Ermini and Casagli (2003), which combines catchment area, dam height and dam volume, but such indices only separate broad classes.

Outburst floods

Breaching releases a flood far larger than any meteorological flood in the same river. Peak discharge depends on lake volume, lake depth above the breach base, breach erosion rate and downstream valley geometry. For engineered embankment dams, Froehlich's (1995) regression relates peak outflow to the volume of water released Vw (m³) and the head hw (m); landslide dams are coarser and more heterogeneous, so such relations are often upper bounds. Physically based breach models (for example the USGS-developed and US National Weather Service methods, and more recent models that couple sediment transport and hydraulics) are preferred for emergency planning when time allows.

Historical outbursts have been devastating. In 1841 the failure of a landslide dam on the Indus below Nanga Parbat, formed during a large landslide, sent a flood hundreds of kilometres downstream, reportedly drowning a Sikh army camped by the river. The 1786 Kangding–Luding earthquake in Sichuan blocked the Dadu River, and the outburst some days later is reported to have killed of order 100,000 people, although the historical figure is uncertain. In 2010 the Attabad landslide in the Hunza valley of Pakistan killed about 20 people and dammed a lake more than 20 km long that drowned a section of the Karakoram Highway and still persists in reduced form.

Management

Emergency options include excavating a spillway to lower the lake in a controlled manner, pumping or siphoning, diversion tunnels, and evacuation downstream combined with real-time lake-level and breach monitoring. The Tangjiashan dam, formed on 12 May 2008 on the Jian River upstream of Beichuan and Mianyang, impounded a lake of roughly 2–3 × 108 m³. Engineers excavated a spillway channel across the dam crest with heavy equipment flown in by helicopter while more than 200,000 people downstream were evacuated; the lake drained through the eroding spillway on 10 June 2008 with a peak discharge reported at about 6,500 m³/s and no deaths. The case is regarded as a model of landslide-dam crisis management.

Glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs) are related phenomena in which moraine-dammed or ice-dammed lakes fail, frequently triggered by an ice or rock avalanche into the lake that creates a displacement wave. Cascades of this type — landslide, wave, dam breach, debris flow — were responsible for the 2013 Kedarnath disaster in India and the 2021 Chamoli disaster, where a rock and ice avalanche of roughly 27 × 106 m³ from Ronti Peak became a debris flow that destroyed two hydropower projects (Shugar et al., 2021).

Exercise

  • (a)
    A landslide dam impounds a lake that will hold 3 × 108 m³ when it overtops. The river delivers 100 m³/s. How long until overtopping, neglecting seepage and evaporation?
  • (b)
    If the dam then breaches through 50 m of head and releases the whole lake, estimate the peak discharge with Froehlich's relation and comment.
Show solution

Answers: (a) t = 3 × 108/100 = 3 × 106 s ≈ 35 days — enough time to excavate a spillway and evacuate, as at Tangjiashan. (b) Qp = 0.607 × (3 × 108)0.295 × 501.24 = 0.607 × 316.8 × 127.9 ≈ 2.5 × 104 m³/s. This is about four times the observed Tangjiashan peak for a similar lake, illustrating both the enormous potential flood and the benefit of a controlled spillway that lowers the lake gradually and limits the breach depth.
