Locating Earthquakes and Source Mechanisms

The location problem

Four unknowns define a hypocentre: origin time t₀ and the three coordinates of x₀. For each station i, the predicted arrival time is t₀ plus the travel time T calculated through a velocity model. Geiger (1910; 1912) introduced the iterative linearised approach still in use. Starting from a trial hypocentre, one computes the residuals Δti = tiobs − tipred and the partial derivatives of T with respect to each hypocentral coordinate, then solves the overdetermined system G Δm = Δt in a least-squares sense and repeats until the solution converges. The model covariance (GᵀG)⁻¹σ² gives formal uncertainties. These usually underestimate the true error, because errors in the velocity model are systematic rather than random.

Several well-known trade-offs limit precision. Depth trades off against origin time when all stations are distant, because both shift arrivals almost uniformly. Depth is therefore best constrained by a station closer than about one focal depth, or by depth phases such as pP and sP for teleseismic events. An event outside the network, with a large azimuthal gap, is poorly located along the direction of the gap. Relative-location methods such as the double-difference algorithm (Waldhauser and Ellsworth, 2000) cancel most of the effect of unmodelled velocity structure by differencing arrival times of closely spaced events at common stations, often using waveform cross-correlation. They can sharpen diffuse clouds of seismicity into well-defined fault planes with errors of tens of metres. Machine-learning phase pickers trained on millions of labelled seismograms (for example PhaseNet and EQTransformer, both published in 2019–2020) now routinely expand catalogues by a factor of 10 or more.

The Wadati diagram

For a uniform VP/VS ratio, plotting the S−P time against the P arrival time at several stations gives a straight line, because tS − tP = (VP/VS − 1)(tP − t₀). The slope gives VP/VS − 1, and the intercept on the time axis gives the origin time. Wadati introduced this diagram in the 1930s, and it remains a quick check on picks and on crustal Poisson's ratio.

Focal mechanisms and the moment tensor

The radiation pattern of a shear dislocation is a double couple. P-wave first motions are compressional in two opposite quadrants and dilatational in the other two, separated by two orthogonal nodal planes: the fault plane and the auxiliary plane. Plotting first-motion polarities on a lower-hemisphere projection produces the familiar "beach ball", which gives the faulting style and the P (compression) and T (tension) axes. Seismological data alone cannot decide which nodal plane is the fault. Aftershock alignments, surface rupture, geodesy or rupture directivity resolve this ambiguity.

The general point source is described by the symmetric seismic moment tensor Mij. For a shear dislocation of area A and average slip D on a fault with normal n and slip direction s, Mij = μAD(sinj + sjni). Since the Centroid Moment Tensor method of Dziewonski, Chou and Woodhouse (1981), long-period waveform inversion has produced moment tensors for essentially every global earthquake above about M5. The Global CMT catalogue begins in 1976. Non-double-couple components point to volcanic, geothermal or collapse sources, complex ruptures, or explosions, and are used in nuclear-test discrimination. Finite-fault inversions of seismic, geodetic and tsunami data now map slip distributions and rupture velocity within hours. Most ruptures propagate at 0.7–0.9 VS. Some strike-slip ruptures, such as Kunlun 2001 and segments of Türkiye 2023, appear to have run at supershear speeds.

Exercise

  • (a)
    Two stations record a local event. At station A, P arrives at 08:00:12.0 and S at 08:00:19.3. At station B, P arrives at 08:00:16.0 and S at 08:00:26.22. Construct the Wadati line and find VP/VS and the origin time.
  • (b)
    Using VP = 6.0 km/s, how far is station A from the hypocentre?
Show solution

Answers: (a) The S−P times are 7.30 s at A and 10.22 s at B. The slope is (10.22 − 7.30)/(16.0 − 12.0) = 0.73, so VP/VS = 1.73. S−P falls to zero at tP = 12.0 − 7.30/0.73 = 12.0 − 10.0 = 2.0 s, so t₀ = 08:00:02.0. Station B gives the same result: 16.0 − 10.22/0.73 = 2.0 s. (b) The P travel time is 12.0 − 2.0 = 10.0 s, so d = 10.0 × 6.0 = 60 km.
