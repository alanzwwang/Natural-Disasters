Earthquake Statistics, Aftershocks and Forecasting

Lesson 6 of 10

Overview12345678910Case studiesReferences

Key idea of this section

log₁₀ N(≥M) = a − b M  (b ≈ 1) ;   n(t) = K / (t + c)p  (p ≈ 1)

The Gutenberg–Richter law

Gutenberg and Richter (1944) found that the cumulative number of earthquakes with magnitude at least M follows log₁₀ N = a − bM. The b-value is close to 1 almost everywhere. Each step down of one magnitude unit therefore brings roughly ten times as many earthquakes, while each step up brings about 32 times more moment, so total moment release is dominated by the largest events. Long-term global averages are about 1 event per year of M ≥ 8, 15 of M 7–7.9, 130–150 of M 6–6.9 and 1,300–1,500 of M 5–5.9. The maximum-likelihood estimator of b (Aki, 1965, with Utsu's correction for binned magnitudes of width ΔM) is

b = log₁₀ e / [ M̄ − (Mc − ΔM/2) ]

where M̄ is the mean magnitude of events at or above the completeness magnitude Mc. Determining Mc correctly is the most important practical step, because an incomplete catalogue biases b downwards. Systematic b-value variations have been linked to differential stress, with lower b at higher stress (Schorlemmer, Wiemer and Wyss, 2005). Whether b-value changes can be used for short-term forecasting remains contested. The distribution must taper or be truncated at some maximum magnitude, since moment release cannot be infinite, but Mmax is poorly constrained for most faults.

Aftershocks

Omori (1894), studying the 1891 Nōbi earthquake, found that aftershock rates decay roughly as 1/t. Utsu (1961) generalised this to n(t) = K/(t + c)p, with p typically 0.9–1.5. Båth's law states that the largest aftershock is typically about 1.2 magnitude units smaller than the mainshock, with considerable scatter. Combining Gutenberg–Richter and Omori–Utsu gives the Reasenberg and Jones (1989) model for the rate of aftershocks of magnitude at least M at time t after a mainshock of magnitude Mm:

λ(t, M) = 10a′ + b(Mm − M) (t + c)−p

Its parameters, updated as the sequence evolves, underpin the operational aftershock forecasts issued by the USGS, New Zealand's GeoNet and others. The Epidemic-Type Aftershock Sequence (ETAS) model (Ogata, 1988) treats every earthquake as able to trigger its own offspring, with productivity rising exponentially with magnitude. It is the standard benchmark for short-term forecasting. Roughly 5 % of earthquakes are followed within days by a larger event in the same area, which makes them foreshocks in retrospect. No reliable way has been found to recognise foreshocks before the mainshock occurs.

Long-term forecasting

Time-independent (Poisson) forecasts give the probability of at least one event in time t as P = 1 − exp(−t/T), where T is the mean recurrence interval. Time-dependent renewal models, using Brownian Passage Time or lognormal distributions, raise or lower this probability according to the time elapsed since the last event. The Uniform California Earthquake Rupture Forecast (UCERF3; Field et al., 2014) combines fault-based and gridded seismicity models. It allows multi-fault ruptures, and its logic tree has thousands of branches. Companion versions add an elastic-rebound-based time dependence (2015) and ETAS-style spatiotemporal clustering (2017).

Prediction, in the sense of specifying time, place and magnitude narrowly enough to justify evacuation, has not been achieved. The 1975 Haicheng, China, evacuation, prompted by a foreshock sequence, is often cited, but it was not repeated: the 1976 Tangshan earthquake struck without a short-term warning. Proposed precursors such as radon emissions, electromagnetic signals, animal behaviour and seismic velocity changes have not survived prospective statistical testing. Prospective testing centres such as the Collaboratory for the Study of Earthquake Predictability (CSEP) now evaluate forecasts against future data. The 2009 L'Aquila case, in which six scientists and an official were convicted of manslaughter in 2012 over risk communication before an Mw 6.3 earthquake that killed 309 people, with the six scientists acquitted on appeal in 2014, showed how difficult it is to communicate low-probability, high-consequence forecasts.

Induced seismicity

Human activity also triggers earthquakes. Mechanisms include fluid injection, which raises pore pressure, as well as reservoir impoundment, mining and gas extraction. Wastewater disposal drove Oklahoma's seismicity from about one or two M ≥ 3 events per year before 2009 to more than 900 in 2015, culminating in the Mw 5.8 Pawnee earthquake of 3 September 2016. Other examples are the 2017 Mw 5.5 Pohang earthquake in South Korea, linked to an enhanced geothermal project, and decades of induced earthquakes in the Groningen gas field in the Netherlands. These led to new regulatory "traffic-light" protocols.

Exercise

  • (a)
    A region with b = 1 records on average 10 earthquakes of M ≥ 5 per year. What is the annual rate of M ≥ 7, and the Poisson probability of at least one M ≥ 7 in 50 years? Repeat for a region with 1 event of M ≥ 5 per year.
  • (b)
    For an aftershock sequence with K = 100 per day, c = 0.05 day and p = 1, how many aftershocks are expected between day 1 and day 10?
  • (c)
    A catalogue complete above Mc = 2.0, with magnitudes reported to 0.1, has a mean magnitude of 2.40. Estimate b.
Show solution

Answers: (a) Rate(M ≥ 7) = 10 × 10−2 = 0.1 per year, and P(50 yr) = 1 − e−5 ≈ 0.993. With 1 event of M ≥ 5 per year the rate is 0.01 per year, and P = 1 − e−0.5 ≈ 0.39. (b) N = K ln[(10 + c)/(1 + c)] = 100 ln(10.05/1.05) ≈ 100 × 2.259 ≈ 226. (c) b = 0.4343 / [2.40 − (2.0 − 0.05)] = 0.4343/0.45 ≈ 0.97. Without the binning correction, the estimate would be 0.4343/0.40 ≈ 1.09.
