Stress, Friction and Fault Mechanics

Lesson 2 of 10

Key idea of this section

Coulomb failure: τ ≥ μ (σn − p) + C ;   ΔCFS = Δτ + μ′ Δσn

Andersonian faulting

Because the Earth's surface is free of shear traction, one principal stress there must be vertical. Anderson (1905; 1951) used this to classify faults. Normal faults occur when the vertical stress is the greatest principal stress σ₁. Thrust faults occur when it is the least, σ₃. Strike-slip faults occur when it is the intermediate stress σ₂. With a friction coefficient near 0.6–0.85, optimally oriented faults lie about 25–30° from σ₁, so normal faults typically dip near 60° and thrusts near 30°. Many large faults are misoriented with respect to the present stress field and are still active, which is evidence that they are weak (see below).

Byerlee's law and effective stress

Laboratory friction experiments on a very wide range of rock types converge on a narrow band of behaviour (Byerlee, 1978):

τ = 0.85 σn                 for σn < 200 MPa
τ = 50 MPa + 0.6 σn         for 200 < σn < 1,700 MPa

The exceptions are mainly clay-rich and phyllosilicate gouges such as smectite and talc, with μ ≈ 0.1–0.3. Pore fluid pressure p reduces the normal stress clamping the fault (Terzaghi's effective-stress principle). Hubbert and Rubey (1959) used this to explain the motion of large, low-angle thrust sheets. With hydrostatic pore pressure, the effective vertical stress at 10 km is about 167 MPa, and Byerlee friction then implies shear strengths around 100 MPa. Heat-flow measurements around the San Andreas, however, show no frictional heating anomaly, which implies average shear stresses of perhaps 10–20 MPa or less. This is the "heat-flow paradox" or "strength of faults" debate. Explanations include intrinsically weak gouge, high pore pressure, and dynamic weakening during rapid slip, such as flash heating, thermal pressurisation of pore fluid and melting. In high-speed friction experiments at slip rates of about 1 m/s, μ can fall to 0.1–0.3 or lower.

Coulomb stress transfer

An earthquake changes the stress on nearby faults. The change in Coulomb failure stress on a receiver fault of given orientation is

ΔCFS = Δτ + μ′ Δσn

Here Δτ is the change in shear stress in the slip direction, Δσn is the change in normal stress (taken as positive for unclamping), and μ′ is an effective friction coefficient (typically 0.4) that absorbs pore-pressure effects. Positive ΔCFS brings a fault closer to failure. Following the 1992 Landers earthquake, King, Stein and Lin (1994) showed that aftershocks were concentrated in regions of positive ΔCFS, and stress changes as small as about 0.01–0.1 MPa (0.1–1 bar) appear to influence seismicity rates. The sequence of large earthquakes that progressed westward along the North Anatolian Fault from 1939 to 1999 is the best-known example of a fault-to-fault cascade consistent with stress transfer (Stein, Barka and Dieterich, 1997). Dynamic stresses from passing seismic waves can also trigger earthquakes at great distances. Remote triggering after Landers was observed as far away as Yellowstone, about 1,250 km.

Rate-and-state friction

Static and dynamic friction coefficients alone cannot explain why faults lock, slip slowly or fail seismically. The empirical rate-and-state laws of Dieterich (1979) and Ruina (1983) describe friction as a function of slip velocity V and a state variable θ that represents the maturity of asperity contacts:

μ = μ₀ + a ln(V/V₀) + b ln(V₀ θ / Dc)
dθ/dt = 1 − V θ / Dc            (ageing law)

Here a and b are dimensionless constants of order 0.01 and Dc is a characteristic slip distance, about 1–100 μm in the laboratory and probably millimetres to centimetres on natural faults. At steady state, μss = μ₀ + (a − b) ln(V/V₀). If a − b > 0 the material is velocity-strengthening, and it slides stably: creeping faults and the shallow, unconsolidated parts of megathrusts behave this way. If a − b < 0 it is velocity-weakening, and it can stick-slip. For a spring-slider of stiffness k, sliding becomes unstable when

k < kc = σn′ (b − a) / Dc

The size of the smallest patch that can nucleate an earthquake scales as μDc/[σn′(b − a)]. The framework explains the depth limits of seismicity, interseismic healing, the slow slip events and tectonic tremor found at the base of many subduction zones since about 2000, and the time-dependent earthquake nucleation that underlies Dieterich's (1994) model of aftershock rates. Marone (1998) and Scholz (2019) give comprehensive reviews.

Stress drop

The static stress drop Δσ is the average reduction in shear stress on the rupture. It scales with average slip D̄ divided by a rupture dimension. For a circular crack of radius r (Eshelby, 1957):

Δσ = (7π/16) μ D̄ / r = 7 M₀ / (16 r³)

Stress drops estimated from seismic spectra are remarkably independent of magnitude over at least 10 magnitude units. They cluster around 1–10 MPa, with large scatter. This self-similarity, with D̄ proportional to rupture length, is why rupture area roughly scales as 10M − 4 km².

Exercise

  • (a)
    Take crustal density 2,700 kg/m³, water density 1,000 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s². Find the lithostatic stress, the hydrostatic pore pressure and the effective vertical stress at 10 km depth, and the shear strength of a fault with μ = 0.6 under that effective normal stress.
  • (b)
    An earthquake changes the stresses on a nearby receiver fault by Δτ = +0.30 MPa and Δσn = −0.20 MPa (clamping). With μ′ = 0.4, find ΔCFS.
  • (c)
    A laboratory fault has σn′ = 50 MPa, b − a = 0.004 and Dc = 20 μm. What is kc?
Show solution

Answers: (a) σv = 2,700 × 9.81 × 10⁴ ≈ 265 MPa; p = 1,000 × 9.81 × 10⁴ ≈ 98 MPa; σv′ ≈ 167 MPa; τ = 0.6 × 167 ≈ 100 MPa. (b) ΔCFS = 0.30 + 0.4 × (−0.20) = +0.22 MPa (2.2 bar). This is well above the roughly 0.1 bar level associated with triggering, so the receiver fault has been brought closer to failure. (c) kc = 50 × 10⁶ × 0.004 / (20 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.0 × 10¹⁰ Pa/m = 10 MPa/mm. A loading frame less stiff than this will produce stick-slip.
