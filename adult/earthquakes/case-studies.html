Case Studies

8 historical events

The following case studies trace how specific earthquakes advanced the science and changed practice. Death tolls are often uncertain, especially for historical events and for disasters in states with weak record-keeping or politically sensitive counts, so ranges are given.

1755 Lisbon, Portugal — the first modern disaster

On the morning of 1 November 1755, All Saints' Day, a great earthquake struck offshore of southwest Iberia. Its magnitude is estimated at about Mw 8.5–9.0 from intensity data and tsunami observations. The source is still debated: candidates include the Gorringe Bank, the Marquês de Pombal and Horseshoe thrust faults, and an incipient subduction zone beneath the Gulf of Cadiz. Shaking lasted several minutes and destroyed much of Lisbon. A tsunami about 30–40 minutes later, with heights of roughly 5–15 m along the Portuguese and Moroccan coasts, swept the Tagus waterfront, and fires burned for about five days. Estimates of deaths in Lisbon range from about 20,000 to 50,000 or more, with additional thousands in Spain and Morocco. Older figures of 60,000–100,000 are probably too high, but the uncertainty is large.

The disaster was intellectually transformative. It provoked Voltaire's "Poème sur le désastre de Lisbonne" (1756) and "Candide" (1759) and wider debate on optimism and theodicy. The chief minister, later the Marquis of Pombal, sent a questionnaire to every parish asking about the duration of shaking, aftershocks, the behaviour of wells and the damage. This is often called the first systematic macroseismic survey. The rebuilt Baixa district introduced the "gaiola pombalina", a timber cage braced with diagonal members inside masonry walls, which is regarded as one of the first engineered earthquake-resistant building systems. John Michell (1760) proposed that earthquakes are waves propagating through the Earth and attempted to locate the source from arrival times.

1906 San Francisco, California — elastic rebound

At 05:12 local time on 18 April 1906, the northern San Andreas Fault ruptured over about 470 km, from near San Juan Bautista to Cape Mendocino. The earthquake is estimated at Mw 7.8–7.9, with right-lateral offsets of 4–6 m in Marin County. The shaking lasted about 45–60 seconds in San Francisco. Damage was greatest on filled marshland and former bay margins, an early observation of site effects. Broken gas mains started fires, broken water mains disabled fire-fighting, and the fires burned for three days, consuming about 28,000 buildings over roughly 490 city blocks. More than half of the population of about 400,000 was made homeless. The official death toll of about 375–500 was later shown to be a substantial undercount. Modern research suggests around 3,000 or more deaths.

The State Earthquake Investigation Commission under Andrew Lawson published a landmark report (Lawson et al., 1908), mapping the rupture and collecting intensity data. Its second volume contained Reid's elastic-rebound theory (Reid, 1910). The 1906 earthquake also yielded some of the first useful teleseismic records, and it remains the reference scenario for hazard planning in the San Francisco Bay Area. Today the USGS estimates a 72 % probability of at least one M ≥ 6.7 earthquake in the region between 2014 and 2043 (UCERF3).

1960 Valdivia, Chile — the largest earthquake recorded

On 22 May 1960 at 15:11 local time (19:11 UTC), the Nazca–South America megathrust ruptured over about 800–1,000 km of southern Chile. A sequence of strong foreshocks had begun the previous day, including an event of about M 8 near Concepción. The seismic moment, about 2 × 10²³ N·m, gives Mw ≈ 9.5 (Kanamori, 1977). Estimates range from 9.4 to 9.6, and the average slip was some 20 m or more. Coastal land subsided by up to about 2 m around Valdivia, permanently drowning farmland and forests, while offshore islands rose. Death tolls are uncertain, from about 1,000 to over 6,000; a figure of around 1,655 is often cited. The Pacific-wide tsunami killed 61 people in Hilo, Hawaii, about 15 hours later and about 140 in Japan about 22 hours later. The Cordón Caulle volcano erupted two days after the mainshock, and landslides dammed the outlet of Lake Riñihue, threatening Valdivia until engineers lowered the dam.

Scientifically, the earthquake produced the first clear observations of the Earth's free oscillations, including the gravest modes with periods near 54 minutes, which provided powerful new constraints on Earth structure. Together with Plafker's (1965) mapping of coseismic uplift and subsidence after the 1964 Alaska earthquake (Mw 9.2, 131 deaths, most from tsunamis), it established that great earthquakes are ruptures of gently dipping subduction megathrusts. The tsunami also prompted the creation in 1965 of the international Pacific Tsunami Warning System, coordinated through UNESCO's Intergovernmental Oceanographic Commission.

1985 Michoacán and Mexico City — site amplification

On 19 September 1985 at 07:17 local time, an Mw 8.0 subduction earthquake occurred off the Pacific coast of Michoacán. An Mw 7.5–7.6 aftershock followed the next evening. Damage near the coast was moderate. In Mexico City, however, about 350–400 km away, hundreds of buildings collapsed and thousands more were damaged. The official death toll was about 5,000–10,000, and some unofficial estimates are much higher.

The explanation lies in the city's geology. Much of central Mexico City is built on soft lacustrine clays of the former Lake Texcoco, tens of metres thick, with very high water content and VS of only about 50–80 m/s. At the SCT station in the lake zone, PGA reached about 0.16–0.17 g with nearly harmonic motion at a dominant period of about 2 s and a long duration. At the University (UNAM) station on hill-zone rock, PGA was only about 0.03–0.04 g, so spectral amplification at 2 s exceeded a factor of 10. Buildings of roughly 6–15 storeys, whose periods were lengthened towards 2 s by softening during shaking, suffered most. The disaster led to a revised building code in 1987, microzonation of the city into hill, transition and lake zones with separate design spectra, and the creation of the Seismic Alert System (SASMEX), which began operating in 1991. On the anniversary, 19 September 2017, an Mw 7.1 intraslab earthquake near Puebla killed about 370 people. That event was much closer, and caused shorter-period damage concentrated in the transition zone.

1995 Kobe (Hyōgo-ken Nanbu), Japan — a modern city at close range

At 05:46 local time on 17 January 1995, an Mw 6.9 (JMA magnitude 7.3) strike-slip earthquake ruptured the Nojima fault on Awaji Island and faults beneath the city of Kobe. Forward directivity produced near-fault velocity pulses exceeding 1 m/s and PGA of about 0.8 g at the JMA Kobe station. Shaking reached JMA intensity 7 in a narrow belt, the first time that level had been assigned. Some 6,434 people died. Most were crushed in older wooden houses with heavy tile roofs, or in pre-1981 non-ductile concrete buildings. More than 100,000 buildings were destroyed. Spectacular failures included the toppling of a 635 m section of the elevated Hanshin Expressway and liquefaction and lateral spreading of the reclaimed Port Island and Rokko Island, which crippled what had been one of the world's busiest container ports. Fires, especially in Nagata Ward, destroyed several thousand more buildings. Direct losses were about 10 trillion yen, roughly US$100 billion at the time.

Kobe drove major reforms. Japan built the dense K-NET strong-motion and Hi-net borehole networks (from 1996), creating some of the densest seismic networks in the world, which later enabled the national EEW system. It also established the Headquarters for Earthquake Research Promotion and began national probabilistic hazard maps. The earthquake accelerated retrofit of pre-1981 buildings, revised bridge design standards, and led to the construction of the E-Defense full-scale shake table, the world's largest. It also transformed volunteerism and disaster management in Japan.

2010 Haiti — vulnerability as the decisive factor

On 12 January 2010 at 16:53 local time, an Mw 7.0 earthquake struck at about 13 km depth, roughly 25 km west-southwest of Port-au-Prince. It was initially attributed to the Enriquillo–Plantain Garden fault zone, but geodetic and geological studies showed that most slip occurred on a previously unmapped, blind oblique-reverse fault (the Léogâne fault) with no surface rupture. Shaking in Port-au-Prince was probably around MMI VIII–IX. The death toll is highly uncertain and politically contested. The Haitian government's figure rose to about 316,000, while independent survey-based estimates range from roughly 46,000–85,000 to about 160,000. An estimated 1.5 million people were displaced. The capital's building stock consisted largely of poorly confined concrete-block masonry with heavy concrete floors and no enforced code.

Haiti illustrates that risk is controlled by vulnerability and exposure more than by magnitude. The 2010 Chile earthquake six weeks later released roughly 500 times more moment (Mw 8.8) but killed about 525 people. Historical records show destructive earthquakes in the same region in 1751 and 1770, yet the long quiet interval had erased institutional memory. Recovery was hampered by the destruction of government capacity and by a cholera epidemic, introduced in October 2010 by United Nations peacekeepers, that killed about 10,000 people. Rapid post-event remote sensing, crowd-mapping (OpenStreetMap) and the USGS PAGER system came of age during the response.

2011 Tōhoku-oki, Japan — the unexpected giant

At 14:46 JST on 11 March 2011, the megathrust between the subducting Pacific plate and the Okhotsk (or North American) plate off northeast Honshu ruptured over about 400–500 km along strike and about 200 km down dip, producing an Mw 9.0–9.1 earthquake. Slip near the trench reached about 50 m or more, far beyond what earlier models of the Japan Trench had allowed. National hazard assessments had anticipated M 7.5–8.2 events on individual segments (Simons et al., 2011). The coastal GNSS station at Oshika moved about 5.3 m east-southeast and subsided about 1.2 m. The resulting tsunami reached a maximum run-up of about 40 m at Miyako and overtopped many seawalls designed for smaller events. About 15,900 people died and about 2,500 remain missing. Including disaster-related deaths, the total exceeds 22,000. The tsunami caused the meltdowns at Fukushima Daiichi nuclear power plant, and total economic losses were on the order of US$200–300 billion, the costliest natural disaster on record at the time.

Japan's EEW system issued a public alert about 8 s after the first P detection, giving roughly 15–30 s of warning in much of the Tōhoku region. Because the rupture was treated as a much smaller point source, the predicted shaking in the Kantō region was too low and no public alert was issued for Tokyo. No passenger-carrying Shinkansen train derailed. The initial magnitude estimate (M 7.9), however, led to tsunami warnings that were too low in the first critical minutes. Sedimentary evidence of the AD 869 Jōgan tsunami had indicated that comparable events had happened before. The lessons were that hazard models must consider rupture of multiple segments and the shallow, near-trench part of the megathrust, and that palaeo-tsunami and geodetic evidence must be weighted properly. After the disaster, Japan deployed the S-net and DONET seafloor cabled observatories.

2023 Kahramanmaraş, Türkiye–Syria — a multi-fault doublet

On 6 February 2023 at 04:17 local time (01:17 UTC), an Mw 7.8 earthquake nucleated on the Narlı splay and propagated onto the East Anatolian Fault. It ruptured roughly 300–350 km of the fault with left-lateral surface offsets of up to about 7–8 m. About nine hours later, at 13:24 local time, an Mw 7.5–7.7 earthquake ruptured the separate Çardak–Sürgü fault system to the north for some 150 km. Parts of the first rupture may have propagated at supershear speeds. Strong-motion records exceeded 1 g in several cities. More than 50,000 people died in Türkiye and about 6,000–8,500 in Syria. Hundreds of thousands of buildings were destroyed or damaged beyond repair across 11 Turkish provinces. The World Bank estimated direct physical damage in Türkiye at about US$34 billion, and Turkish government estimates of total cost exceeded US$100 billion.

Many collapsed buildings had been constructed after the 1999 Kocaeli (İzmit) earthquake prompted code reforms, and some had benefited from construction amnesties that legalised non-compliant buildings. Investigations pointed to poor concrete quality, inadequate transverse reinforcement, soft storeys and weak enforcement, while buildings designed and built to the 2018 code generally performed well. The sequence demonstrated the importance of multi-fault rupture scenarios, the potential for a second large shock to strike within hours, and the gulf between code provisions and their implementation. In north-western Syria, where conflict had already degraded infrastructure, international aid was severely delayed. Coulomb stress analyses suggested that the first event loaded the fault of the second. The sequence is now one of the best-instrumented large continental strike-slip events, with dense strong-motion, InSAR and optical image correlation data.
