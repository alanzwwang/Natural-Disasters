Volatiles, Vesiculation and Fragmentation

Lesson 3 of 10

Overview12345678910Case studiesReferences

Key idea of this section

Dissolved water and CO₂ come out of solution as pressure falls; when the expanding gas fraction or strain rate becomes large enough, the melt breaks into fragments and an explosive eruption begins

Volatile species and solubility

The principal magmatic volatiles are H₂O (typically 0.1–0.5 wt% in ridge basalts, 2–6 wt% in arc magmas, and up to ~6–7 wt% in some rhyolites), CO₂, sulfur species (SO₂, H₂S), and halogens (HCl, HF). Water solubility in silicate melts increases approximately with the square root of pressure, reflecting dissolution of water partly as hydroxyl groups. For rhyolite a useful approximation is:

c = s P1/2,    s ≈ 4.1 × 10⁻⁶ Pa−1/2

where c is the mass fraction of dissolved water. This gives about 4 wt% at 100 MPa (~4 km depth) and about 6 wt% at 200 MPa. CO₂ is far less soluble — of order a thousand parts per million at hundreds of MPa — so it exsolves at much greater depth, and the deep CO₂ flux can provide early warning of fresh magma (as at Kīlauea and Etna). Sulfur solubility depends strongly on oxidation state and on the presence of sulfide or anhydrite.

Nucleation and bubble growth

When magma rises above its saturation pressure, it becomes supersaturated and bubbles nucleate — homogeneously at high supersaturation (of order 100 MPa or more in crystal-poor rhyolite) or heterogeneously on crystals such as Fe–Ti oxides at much lower supersaturation. Bubbles then grow by diffusion of volatiles from the melt and by decompression expansion. In low-viscosity basalt, bubbles can rise relative to the melt, coalesce, and separate from it, producing Strombolian bursts of large gas slugs; in viscous silicic magma, bubbles remain trapped and grow together with the melt (closed-system degassing) until the gas fraction becomes very large.

The gas volume fraction

For a mass fraction n of exsolved water at pressure P and temperature T, treated as an ideal gas with molar mass M = 0.018 kg mol⁻¹, the volume of gas per kilogram of magma is n R T / (M P), and the gas volume fraction is:

α = (n R T / M P) / (n R T / M P + 1/ρmelt)

Because P falls by orders of magnitude in the upper few kilometres, α rises rapidly; even a few weight per cent of water produces gas volume fractions above 99% at atmospheric pressure. This is the root of explosive volcanism.

Fragmentation criteria

Fragmentation is the transition from a continuous melt containing bubbles to a continuous gas carrying fragments of melt (pyroclasts). Several criteria have been proposed and are probably all relevant in different settings. The classical volume-fraction criterion (Sparks, 1978) places fragmentation where α reaches ~0.75–0.8, near the limit of bubble packing; many pumices indeed have vesicularities of 70–85%. The strain-rate criterion (Papale, 1999) states that magma fragments when the elongational strain rate exceeds the inverse of its structural relaxation time, i.e. when the melt responds as a brittle solid (a glass transition crossed by deformation rate rather than temperature). The overpressure criterion holds that bubble walls fail when the gas overpressure exceeds the tensile strength of the melt (of order a few MPa), which shock-tube experiments confirm. Permeability plays a crucial competing role: if bubbles connect and gas escapes through the magma or conduit walls (open-system degassing), the magma can erupt effusively as lava domes even when initially volatile-rich. The balance between ascent rate and outgassing is therefore a central control on whether a silicic eruption is explosive or effusive.

Magma–water interaction

External water adds a second explosive pathway. Phreatomagmatic eruptions result from rapid heat transfer from magma to groundwater, lake or sea water, generating steam explosions and very fine ash; molten fuel–coolant interaction can achieve conversion efficiencies of thermal to mechanical energy of several per cent or more. Phreatic (steam-driven) eruptions involve no fresh magma at the surface and can occur with very little warning, as at Ontake (Japan) in 2014, which killed 63 hikers, and Whakaari/White Island (New Zealand) in 2019, which killed 22.

Exercise

  • (a)
    Using c = s P1/2 with s = 4.1 × 10⁻⁶ Pa−1/2, at what pressure is a rhyolite containing 5 wt% water saturated? At what depth is this, for a crustal density of 2,400 kg m⁻³?
  • (b)
    At 10 MPa and 900 °C, what gas volume fraction does this magma have (melt density 2,300 kg m⁻³)? Has it passed the volume-fraction fragmentation threshold?
Show solution

Answers: (a) P = (c/s)² = (0.05 / 4.1 × 10⁻⁶)² ≈ 1.49 × 10⁸ Pa ≈ 149 MPa; depth ≈ 1.49 × 10⁸ / (2,400 × 9.81) ≈ 6.3 km. (b) Dissolved water at 10 MPa is 4.1 × 10⁻⁶ × √(10⁷) ≈ 0.013, so about n ≈ 0.037 of the magma mass is exsolved gas (neglecting the small correction for the changing melt mass). Gas volume per kg: 0.037 × 8.314 × 1,173 / (0.018 × 10⁷) ≈ 2.0 × 10⁻³ m³; melt volume per kg: 1/2,300 ≈ 4.35 × 10⁻⁴ m³. Hence α ≈ 2.0 / (2.0 + 0.435) ≈ 0.82, above the 0.75–0.8 threshold, so fragmentation is expected at or slightly below this level in a closed system. At 0.1 MPa the same calculation gives α ≈ 0.998, with gas occupying about 600 times the melt volume.
