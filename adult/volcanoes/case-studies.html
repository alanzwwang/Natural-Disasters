Case Studies

The eruptions below have shaped modern volcanology. Each illustrates a distinct combination of mechanism and impact, and each changed scientific understanding or practice. Figures quoted are those of the main scientific syntheses; where they are uncertain or disputed, ranges are given.

AD 79 Vesuvius, Italy — the archetypal Plinian eruption

The eruption of Vesuvius (then the volcano known as Somma–Vesuvius) followed centuries of dormancy and a destructive earthquake in AD 62/63. The traditional date of 24 August comes from manuscript copies of Pliny the Younger's letters to the historian Tacitus, but archaeological evidence — autumn fruits, heavier clothing, and a charcoal inscription found at Pompeii in 2018 — suggests a date around 24 October is more likely; the question remains debated.

The eruption began with a phreatomagmatic opening phase, then a Plinian phase in which a column of about 26–33 km dispersed phonolitic pumice to the south-east. Pompeii, ~10 km from the vent, was buried beneath 2–3 m of pumice fall over roughly 18 hours, which caused roofs to collapse. The column then became unstable and collapsed, sending a series of pyroclastic density currents across the region; at Herculaneum, only ~7 km to the west, the currents killed people sheltering in boathouses on the shore (the remains of more than 300 people were found there from the 1980s onwards) and buried the town under ~20 m of deposits. Total erupted volume is estimated at a few km³ DRE. The total death toll is unknown; more than 1,100 bodies have been recovered at Pompeii, and estimates of total fatalities across the region run to a few thousand or more. Pliny the Elder, commander of the fleet at Misenum, died at Stabiae while attempting a rescue.

Pliny the Younger's description of the column, "like an umbrella pine", gave its name to the Plinian style, and stratigraphic studies of the deposits by Sigurdsson, Carey and colleagues in the 1980s became a textbook example of column collapse and PDC dynamics. Vesuvius last erupted in 1944, and its red zone today contains ~600,000–700,000 people, making it one of the world's highest-risk volcanoes.

1783–1784 Laki, Iceland — a flood-basalt eruption in miniature

Between 8 June 1783 and 7 February 1784, a 27-km-long fissure system of about 140 vents (Lakagígar) erupted roughly 14.7 km³ of basaltic lava covering ~600 km², in ten eruptive episodes each beginning with explosive activity and lava fountains. The eruption released an estimated 122 Tg of SO₂ (Thordarson and Self, 2003), about 95 Tg of it into the upper troposphere and lower stratosphere, as well as large quantities of fluorine and chlorine.

In Iceland, fluorine poisoning of grass and the effects of the acid haze killed about 50% of cattle and horses and roughly 80% of sheep; the ensuing "Haze Famine" (Móðuharðindin) killed about 20–25% of the population (roughly 9,000–10,000 people). A sulfuric "dry fog" spread across Europe during summer 1783; contemporary accounts describe respiratory illness, crop damage and unusual heat, followed by a severe winter in 1783–1784. Studies of parish registers in England suggest excess mortality of the order of 10,000–20,000 people in summer and autumn 1783, plausibly linked to air pollution, although attribution is uncertain.

Laki demonstrates that effusive, low-VEI eruptions can have extreme atmospheric and societal impact. A similar eruption today would produce sustained air-quality hazards over Europe and major disruption of aviation and agriculture, which is why the UK has included a Laki-type eruption on its National Risk Register. Smaller, analogous effects occurred during the 2014–2015 Holuhraun eruption (Bárðarbunga), which released ~11 Tg of SO₂.

1815 Tambora, Indonesia — the largest eruption in recorded history

Tambora, on Sumbawa, reawakened in 1812 after centuries of quiescence. A preliminary explosion occurred on 5 April 1815; the climax on 10–11 April produced Plinian columns of up to ~43 km, then collapsed into pyroclastic density currents that swept to the sea, destroying the small kingdoms of Tambora and Sanggar. The summit lost ~1.5 km of height (from an estimated ~4,300 m to ~2,850 m) and a caldera about 6 km wide and ~1.1 km deep formed. Estimates of erupted volume range from ~30–33 km³ DRE (Sigurdsson and Carey, 1989) to ~41 km³ DRE (Kandlbauer and Sparks, 2014), placing it at VEI 7 and magnitude ~7. Explosions were heard more than 2,000 km away.

Direct deaths are estimated at about 10,000–12,000; famine and disease on Sumbawa and Lombok raised the total to perhaps 70,000–90,000 or more (Oppenheimer, 2003). Stratospheric sulfur injection is estimated at roughly 50–60 Tg of SO₂ — about three times Pinatubo — and the following year, 1816, became the "Year Without a Summer" in Europe and North America: snow fell in New England in June, harvests failed widely, grain prices soared and famine and typhus spread in parts of Europe. Northern Hemisphere summer temperatures were perhaps 0.4–0.7 °C below average, with much larger regional anomalies.

Tambora demonstrated the global reach of large eruptions, though the link between the eruption and 1816 weather was established only in the 20th century. It is a key benchmark for the effect of a future VEI 7 eruption on a modern, globalised society.

1883 Krakatau, Indonesia — the sound and the sea

Krakatau, in the Sunda Strait between Java and Sumatra, became active in May 1883. The climactic phase on 26–27 August culminated in four great explosions on the morning of 27 August, the largest at about 10:02 local time. The explosion was heard about 4,800 km away on Rodrigues Island in the Indian Ocean, and its atmospheric pressure wave was recorded by barographs around the world for several days as it circled the globe repeatedly. Most of the island collapsed into a submarine caldera. Erupted volume estimates are of order 10–20 km³ of bulk tephra (VEI 6), although they are uncertain.

Tsunamis, with run-up locally exceeding 30 m, devastated the coasts of the Sunda Strait and killed most of the roughly 36,000 victims (the official Dutch figure was 36,417). PDCs travelled across ~40 km of sea to reach Sumatra, killing perhaps a thousand or more people. The mechanism of the tsunamis — PDCs entering the sea, caldera collapse, or phreatomagmatic explosions — is still debated. Aerosols caused vivid red sunsets around the world and a measurable global cooling of a few tenths of a degree.

The Royal Society report of 1888 assembled worldwide observations, making Krakatau the first eruption studied as a global geophysical event, aided by the new telegraph network. Anak Krakatau ("child of Krakatau") emerged in 1927, and its flank collapse on 22 December 2018 generated a tsunami that killed more than 400 people, underscoring the persistence of volcano-tsunami hazard.

1980 Mount St. Helens, USA — the lateral blast

Mount St. Helens awoke on 20 March 1980 with an M4.2 earthquake, followed by phreatic explosions. Intrusion of a cryptodome of dacitic magma caused the north flank to bulge outward at up to ~1.5–2.5 m per day, displacing it by more than 140 m. At 8:32 a.m. PDT on 18 May, an earthquake of magnitude ~5.1 triggered the collapse of the bulge as a debris avalanche of ~2.5 km³, the largest landslide in recorded history. Sudden unloading depressurised the cryptodome and hydrothermal system, producing a lateral blast that devastated ~600 km² of forest, reaching speeds over 480 km/h (estimates range up to ~1,000 km/h). A Plinian column then rose to ~24 km and continued for about nine hours, while PDCs and lahars, especially down the Toutle River, destroyed bridges, homes and roads.

The eruption killed 57 people, including volcanologist David Johnston at an observation post ~10 km north of the vent. Total erupted volume (excluding the avalanche) was about 1 km³ bulk (VEI 5), and the summit lost ~400 m of height. Ash fell over eleven US states. The total economic loss was estimated at about US$1.1 billion (1980 dollars).

Mount St. Helens revolutionised understanding of edifice collapse and lateral blasts, and led to recognition that many volcanoes worldwide are built over prehistoric collapse deposits (hummocky terrain). It drove the expansion of the USGS Volcano Hazards Program and the Cascades Volcano Observatory, and its later dome-building phases (1980–1986, 2004–2008) became testing grounds for forecasting methods.

1985 Nevado del Ruiz, Colombia — a preventable catastrophe

Nevado del Ruiz, a glacier-capped stratovolcano in the Colombian Andes, showed unrest throughout 1985, including a phreatic eruption in September. A hazard map completed in early October correctly showed that lahars could reach Armero, a town in the valley of the Lagunillas River. On the evening of 13 November 1985, a small Plinian eruption (VEI 3, a few tens of million m³ of magma) began at about 21:08 local time. PDCs and ballistic ejecta scoured and melted part of the summit ice cap — perhaps 10% of its area — producing meltwater that generated lahars which swept down several drainages. Gaining volume by erosion, the lahars reached Armero, some 70 km downstream, at around 23:30, more than two hours after the eruption began. About 23,000 people were killed, most of them in Armero, where roughly three-quarters of the inhabitants died.

Failures of communication, uncertain responsibility, the absence of a clear chain of command, and reluctance to order an evacuation that might prove unnecessary combined to turn a moderate eruption into the second-deadliest volcanic disaster of the 20th century. The analysis by Voight (1990) remains a classic study of the "human factors" of volcanic crises.

Armero transformed international volcanology. It led to the creation of the USGS–USAID Volcano Disaster Assistance Program in 1986, the development of acoustic flow monitors for lahars, and an emphasis on risk communication, hazard education and scientist–authority relationships that proved decisive at Pinatubo six years later.

1991 Pinatubo, Philippines — a forecasting success

Pinatubo, on Luzon, had not erupted for about 500 years and was not widely recognised as dangerous. Phreatic explosions on 2 April 1991 prompted the Philippine Institute of Volcanology and Seismology (PHIVOLCS) and the USGS to install seismometers, tiltmeters and gas-measuring equipment. SO₂ emissions rose from ~500 t/day in May to over 5,000 t/day by late May, then fell — suggesting sealing of the system — while seismicity migrated beneath the summit and a dome appeared on 7 June. A five-level alert system was used, and evacuation zones were progressively extended to 20 and then 30 km, eventually involving some 60,000 local residents and about 15,000 personnel of the US Clark Air Base.

The climactic eruption on 15 June 1991 lasted about nine hours, coinciding with Typhoon Yunya. It produced a column of ~35 km (reaching ~40 km at its peak), an umbrella cloud several hundred kilometres across, and ignimbrites that filled valleys to depths of up to ~200 m. It erupted ~8–10 km³ of bulk tephra (~3.7–5.3 km³ DRE; Newhall and Punongbayan, 1996) and formed a caldera ~2.5 km wide. About 300–350 people died in the eruption, mostly from roof collapses under typhoon-soaked ash, and several hundred more in subsequent lahars and from disease in evacuation camps; the USGS estimated that forecasts and evacuations saved at least 5,000 lives and at least US$250 million in property. Lahars remobilised fresh deposits for more than a decade, burying towns such as Bacolor.

Pinatubo's ~15–20 Tg of SO₂ produced the most closely observed volcanic aerosol cloud in history. Global surface temperature fell by about 0.4–0.5 °C in 1992; predictions made by climate models before the cooling occurred (Hansen et al., 1992) provided an important test of climate sensitivity.

2022 Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai, Tonga — a submarine giant

Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai is a largely submarine caldera volcano whose rim had formed two small islands, joined by new land in a Surtseyan eruption in 2014–2015. After weeks of activity, a cataclysmic explosive eruption on 15 January 2022 — the climax beginning at about 04:15 UTC — generated a plume that reached ~57 km, into the mesosphere, the highest ever recorded by satellite. Detected global atmospheric waves included a Lamb wave that travelled around the Earth several times at ~310–320 m s⁻¹ and was recorded on barometers worldwide, much like Krakatau in 1883, and associated ionospheric disturbances. Lightning flash rates were the highest ever observed in any storm.

Tsunamis devastated parts of Tonga and were recorded across the Pacific and beyond, including waves arriving earlier than conventional tsunami travel times would predict, generated by coupling of the atmospheric pressure wave to the ocean. Volcanic density currents on the seafloor, travelling at ~120 km/h, destroyed telecommunications cables, cutting Tonga off from the world for weeks. Remarkably, deaths were few: about four in Tonga and two in Peru, where people drowned in waves on the coast ~10,000 km away. The eruption is usually rated VEI 5–6; seabed surveys show the caldera floor deepened by hundreds of metres and that several cubic kilometres of material were displaced.

The eruption injected unprecedented amounts of water into the stratosphere (~150 Tg; Millán et al., 2022) but little SO₂. It has prompted new research into submarine explosive volcanism, atmosphere–ocean coupling of tsunamis, and the vulnerability of undersea infrastructure.
