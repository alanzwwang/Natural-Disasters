Plate Settings and Magma Genesis

Lesson 1 of 10

Overview12345678910Case studiesReferences

Key idea of this section

The mantle is almost entirely solid; magma forms only where rock crosses its solidus — by decompression, by adding volatiles (flux), or by adding heat

Where volcanoes occur

Roughly three-quarters of the Earth's annual magma production occurs at divergent plate boundaries, where the mid-ocean ridge system generates about 20 km³ of new oceanic crust each year, almost entirely unseen beneath the sea. Subduction zones host most of the subaerial, explosive and dangerous volcanoes — the Pacific "Ring of Fire", the Indonesian (Sunda–Banda) arc, the Lesser Antilles, the Aegean and the Aleutians. Intraplate "hotspot" volcanism (Hawaiʻi, Réunion, Galápagos, Yellowstone) and continental rifts (East Africa, Iceland where a hotspot coincides with a ridge) make up the remainder. Estimates of the global volumetric output of magma are of order 20–35 km³ per year, of which only a small fraction — perhaps one-fifth to one-tenth — reaches the surface; the rest crystallises as intrusions. These figures are uncertain by tens of per cent.

Decompression melting

The upper mantle is peridotite, dominated by olivine with orthopyroxene, clinopyroxene and an aluminous phase. Its dry solidus at the surface is near 1,120 °C and rises with pressure by roughly 130 °C per GPa at low pressures; a commonly used fit (Hirschmann, 2000) is approximately:

Tsolidus (°C) ≈ 1120 + 132.9 P − 5.1 P²      (P in GPa)

Convecting mantle follows an adiabat whose temperature changes only slowly with depth — of order 0.3–0.5 °C per km for solid mantle — anchored by the mantle potential temperature Tp, the temperature the mantle would have if brought adiabatically to the surface without melting, generally estimated at 1,300–1,400 °C beneath ridges and 100–300 °C hotter beneath major hotspots. Because the solidus slope is much steeper than the adiabat, mantle rising beneath a ridge or plume crosses its solidus at some depth and begins to melt. Once melting begins, latent heat is consumed and the temperature follows a shallower path; melt productivity is of order 10–20% per GPa of further decompression (roughly 0.3–0.6% per km). Beneath normal ridges melting begins at 60–80 km and the average melt fraction of the melting column is about 10%, producing an oceanic crust about 6–7 km thick (McKenzie and Bickle, 1988, provided the classic analysis). Hotter plume mantle starts melting deeper, melts more, and builds thicker crust, as in Iceland, where crust reaches 20–40 km.

Flux melting at subduction zones

Subducting oceanic lithosphere carries water in altered crust, sediments and serpentinised mantle. As the slab heats and pressure rises, hydrous minerals (amphibole, lawsonite, chlorite, serpentine) break down and release fluids into the overlying mantle wedge. Water dramatically depresses the peridotite solidus — by several hundred degrees at water saturation — so that the hot core of the wedge melts even without decompression. Arc volcanoes typically sit above the point where the slab reaches about 70–170 km depth, most commonly around 100 km, a remarkably consistent geometry that reflects the combination of slab dehydration and the thermal structure of the wedge. Primary arc magmas are hydrous basalts carrying perhaps 2–6 wt% H₂O, which is why arc volcanoes are far more explosive than ridge or hotspot volcanoes.

Heat-transfer melting and crustal processing

When mantle-derived basalt stalls in the crust, it cools and crystallises, releasing sensible heat and latent heat of crystallisation. That heat can partially melt the surrounding crust, which has a much lower solidus (roughly 650–750 °C for water-bearing granitic compositions). This "heat-transfer" or crustal anatexis process, combined with fractional crystallisation and assimilation (the AFC model), produces the evolved, silica-rich andesites, dacites and rhyolites characteristic of continental arcs and large silicic calderas. Modern work views most crustal magma reservoirs as "trans-crustal mush systems": vertically extensive regions of crystal-rich mush with transient, melt-rich lenses, rather than the classic single, liquid-filled magma chamber.

Why magma rises

Magma rises because it is less dense than its surroundings. Basaltic melt (~2,700–2,800 kg m⁻³ at depth) is lighter than mantle peridotite (~3,300 kg m⁻³), and the buoyancy pressure gradient Δρ g drives porous flow through the partially molten mantle and, at higher melt fractions, fracture propagation as dykes. In the crust, magma may reach a level of neutral buoyancy where its density equals that of the host rock, which is one reason magma reservoirs form at characteristic depths of a few kilometres to ~15 km.

Exercise

  • (a)
    Calculate the lithostatic pressure at 60 km depth, taking a mean density of 3,300 kg m⁻³ and g = 9.81 m s⁻².
  • (b)
    Approximate the solidus as Ts = 1120 + 130 P and the solid adiabat as T = 1350 + 15 P (°C, P in GPa). At what pressure and depth does upwelling mantle begin to melt?
  • (c)
    What buoyancy pressure gradient drives basaltic melt (2,800 kg m⁻³) through mantle of 3,300 kg m⁻³?
Show solution

Answers: (a) P = ρgh = 3,300 × 9.81 × 60,000 ≈ 1.94 × 10⁹ Pa ≈ 1.9 GPa. (b) Setting 1120 + 130P = 1350 + 15P gives 115P = 230, so P = 2.0 GPa, which corresponds to a depth of 2.0 × 10⁹ / (3,300 × 9.81) ≈ 62 km — consistent with the 60–80 km onset of melting beneath ridges. (c) Δρ g = 500 × 9.81 ≈ 4.9 × 10³ Pa m⁻¹, i.e. about 4.9 MPa per km — a small but persistent force acting over tens of kilometres.
