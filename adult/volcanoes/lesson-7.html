Monitoring and Forecasting

Lesson 7 of 10

Overview12345678910Case studiesReferences

Key idea of this section

Magma on the move breaks rock, deforms the ground and releases gas; combining seismic, geodetic and geochemical observations allows probabilistic forecasts of eruptions

Volcano seismology

Seismic monitoring is the backbone of volcano surveillance. Volcano-tectonic (VT) earthquakes are ordinary shear failures (dominant frequencies above ~5 Hz) caused by stress changes around intruding magma. Long-period (LP) events (~0.5–5 Hz) are attributed to resonance of fluid-filled cracks and conduits or to slow failure in weak, shallow material; swarms of LP events preceded the 1985 Nevado del Ruiz eruption and the 1989–1990 Redoubt eruptions, the latter providing a famous successful short-term forecast by Bernard Chouet and colleagues. Very-long-period (VLP) signals (periods of ~2–100 s) reflect mass movements of magma and gas. Volcanic tremor is a continuous signal lasting minutes to months, linked to sustained flow of fluids. Real-time Seismic Amplitude Measurement (RSAM) and Seismic Spectral Amplitude Measurement (SSAM), developed by the USGS, condense seismicity into time series that observatories track continuously. Infrasound arrays detect explosions, including at remote volcanoes, and global networks detected the Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai pressure wave around the world.

Ground deformation

Pressurisation of a magma reservoir inflates the ground surface. The simplest model, attributed to Mogi (1958), treats the reservoir as a point pressure source at depth d in an elastic half-space with Poisson's ratio ν. The vertical surface displacement at radial distance r for a source volume change ΔV is:

uz(r) = (1 − ν) ΔV d / [π (d² + r²)3/2]

so that the maximum uplift above the source is (1 − ν) ΔV / (π d²), and the ratio of horizontal to vertical displacement is r/d. More realistic models include finite spheres, prolate and oblate ellipsoids, dykes and sills (Okada dislocations), topography, crustal heterogeneity and viscoelasticity; the volume change of the reservoir is not equal to the volume of injected magma because magma is compressible.

Deformation is measured by levelling and electronic distance measurement (historically), by borehole and platform tiltmeters (sensitive to sub-microradian tilts), by continuous GNSS (millimetre precision in all three components), and by Interferometric Synthetic Aperture Radar (InSAR). InSAR measures the change in range between two radar acquisitions along the satellite's line of sight; each interferometric fringe corresponds to half a radar wavelength (about 2.8 cm for C-band Sentinel-1 with λ ≈ 5.55 cm). Since the 1990s InSAR has revealed inflation at volcanoes thought to be dormant (e.g. Uturuncu, Bolivia; Laguna del Maule, Chile) and has documented the deflation that accompanies caldera collapse, as at Kīlauea in 2018 and Bárðarbunga in 2014–2015. Not all inflation leads to eruption; only a minority of deforming volcanoes erupt within years, and caldera "unrest" can persist for decades without eruption (Campi Flegrei, Italy; Long Valley, California; Yellowstone).

Gas geochemistry

Gas emissions provide a direct window on degassing magma. SO₂ is measured remotely by ultraviolet spectroscopy — correlation spectrometers (COSPEC) historically, and today by differential optical absorption spectroscopy (DOAS) instruments, scanning networks, UV cameras and satellites (TOMS, OMI, TROPOMI). Flux is obtained by integrating the column amount across the plume cross-section and multiplying by the plume speed. CO₂ and H₂S are measured in situ with Multi-GAS sensors; rising CO₂/SO₂ ratios can indicate deep, fresh magma, whereas falling SO₂ with rising seismicity may indicate sealing of the system and a build-up of pressure. Diffuse soil CO₂ degassing and thermal infrared imaging (from ground and satellite) complete the toolkit.

Forecasting

Forecasting combines observations with models of the eruption process. Voight (1988) proposed the Failure Forecast Method, based on the empirical relation for materials approaching failure:

d²Ω/dt² = A (dΩ/dt)α

where Ω is an observable such as cumulative seismic energy, strain or tilt. For α = 2, the inverse rate (dΩ/dt)⁻¹ decreases linearly with time and extrapolates to zero at the predicted failure time. The method has succeeded retrospectively and occasionally prospectively (e.g. dome explosions at Mount St. Helens in the 1980s), but often fails because of noise, changing processes, or failure of the system to reach eruption. In practice, observatories use expert elicitation and probabilistic event trees (e.g. BETEF, Bayesian Event Tree for Eruption Forecasting), combining base rates from analogous volcanoes with monitoring data. Alerts are communicated by levels: the USGS ground-based system (Normal, Advisory, Watch, Warning) and the aviation colour code (Green, Yellow, Orange, Red), with many national systems in use worldwide. Notable forecasting successes include Pinatubo (1991), Merapi (2010, where some 350,000–400,000 people were evacuated and many thousands of lives were probably saved despite ~350–400 deaths), and the repeated evacuations at Soufrière Hills, Montserrat. Missed or ambiguous forecasts — Nevado del Ruiz (1985), Ontake (2014), Whakaari (2019) — highlight the difficulty of phreatic eruptions and of communicating uncertainty.

Exercise

  • (a)
    A Mogi source at 4 km depth inflates by ΔV = 10⁷ m³ (ν = 0.25). Calculate the maximum uplift, and the uplift 4 km from the centre. How many C-band InSAR fringes (2.8 cm each) does the maximum uplift represent (ignore line-of-sight geometry)?
  • (b)
    A DOAS traverse 2 km wide below a plume measures a mean SO₂ column of 0.5 g m⁻² and the wind speed is 8 m s⁻¹. What is the SO₂ flux in kg s⁻¹ and tonnes per day?
  • (c)
    The inverse rate of seismic energy release is 0.5 units at t = 0 and 0.3 units at t = 10 days. For α = 2, when does the Failure Forecast Method predict failure?
Show solution

Answers: (a) umax = (1 − 0.25) × 10⁷ / (π × 4,000²) = 7.5 × 10⁶ / (5.03 × 10⁷) ≈ 0.149 m ≈ 15 cm. At r = 4 km: (d² + r²)3/2 = (3.2 × 10⁷)3/2 ≈ 1.81 × 10¹¹, so uz = 0.75 × 10⁷ × 4,000 / (π × 1.81 × 10¹¹) ≈ 0.053 m — about 35% of the peak. Fringes: 0.149 / 0.028 ≈ 5.3. (b) Flux = 0.5 × 10⁻³ kg m⁻² × 2,000 m × 8 m s⁻¹ = 8 kg s⁻¹ = 8 × 86,400 / 1,000 ≈ 690 t/day, a moderate flux typical of an open-vent volcano in unrest. (c) The inverse rate falls by 0.02 per day, so it reaches zero at t = 0.5/0.02 = 25 days — about 15 days after the second observation.
