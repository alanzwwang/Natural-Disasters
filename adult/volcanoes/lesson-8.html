Volcanic Aerosols and Climate

Lesson 8 of 10

Overview12345678910Case studiesReferences

Key idea of this section

Climatic impact depends on sulfur injected into the stratosphere, not on erupted volume; sulfate aerosols reflect sunlight and cool the surface for one to three years

From SO₂ to sulfate aerosol

Volcanic ash falls out of the atmosphere within days to weeks and has little effect on climate. Sulfur gases injected above the tropopause, however, are oxidised by the hydroxyl radical to sulfuric acid, which condenses with water into droplets of roughly 75% H₂SO₄ by mass. For the 1991 Pinatubo eruption the SO₂ cloud had an e-folding lifetime of about 3–5 weeks, and the resulting aerosol layer decayed with an e-folding time of about 12 months. Tropical eruptions spread aerosol into both hemispheres via the Brewer–Dobson circulation; high-latitude eruptions mainly affect their own hemisphere and have shorter aerosol lifetimes. Because the reaction is limited by OH availability, very large SO₂ injections may form larger particles that settle faster and scatter less efficiently per unit mass, a non-linearity that is important for super-eruptions.

Radiative effects

Sulfate aerosols scatter incoming shortwave radiation back to space, cooling the surface, while absorbing outgoing longwave and near-infrared radiation, warming the lower stratosphere. The net top-of-atmosphere effective radiative forcing scales approximately linearly with stratospheric aerosol optical depth (AOD) at 550 nm, with a coefficient of roughly −20 to −25 W m⁻² per unit AOD in recent assessments. Pinatubo injected roughly 15–20 Tg of SO₂, producing a peak global-mean AOD of about 0.1–0.15 and a peak forcing of roughly −3 W m⁻²; global mean surface temperature fell by about 0.4–0.5 °C in 1992. Paradoxically, winter warming over northern Eurasia and North America is often observed after large tropical eruptions, attributed to a strengthened polar vortex and positive North Atlantic Oscillation, though the robustness of this response is debated. Volcanic aerosols also provide surfaces for heterogeneous chemistry that accelerates ozone depletion, as observed after Pinatubo.

Ice cores and the volcanic record

Sulfate and tephra preserved in Greenland and Antarctic ice cores record past eruptions. Bipolar sulfate spikes indicate tropical eruptions; their magnitude allows estimation of stratospheric sulfur loading. Ice-core reconstructions (e.g. Sigl et al., 2015) show that clusters of large eruptions, such as those of the 530s–540s CE and the 1250s–1450s CE, coincide with cold periods, crop failures and societal stress recorded in tree rings and history. The 1257 eruption of Samalas (Rinjani complex, Lombok, Indonesia) produced the largest sulfate signal of the last two millennia. Volcanic forcing is a major contributor to natural climate variability over the past millennium and one of the dominant drivers of the onset of the Little Ice Age.

Water vapour and the Hunga Tonga anomaly

The 2022 Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai eruption injected comparatively little SO₂ (around 0.4–0.5 Tg) but about 150 Tg of water vapour — some 10% of the stratosphere's total water burden — a type of perturbation never previously observed. Water vapour is a greenhouse gas, and its prolonged residence in the stratosphere (several years) exerts a warming influence, while the sulfate aerosol exerted a cooling one; most studies find the net effect on global surface temperature to be small, and several find it slightly negative (net cooling), so the eruption is not considered a significant cause of the record warmth of 2023–2024. Quantification remains an active research topic. The eruption also affected stratospheric chemistry and circulation, reminding us that not all volcanic climate effects are cooling.

Volcanic CO₂ in context

Volcanoes emit CO₂ continuously, but the total from subaerial and submarine volcanism (including diffuse degassing) is estimated at a few hundred million tonnes per year — about 1% or less of anthropogenic emissions (~37 Gt CO₂ per year from fossil fuels and industry). On geological timescales, however, volcanic CO₂ is the main source of atmospheric carbon, and massive flood-basalt provinces (Siberian Traps at ~252 Ma; Central Atlantic Magmatic Province at ~201 Ma; Deccan Traps at ~66 Ma) are implicated in mass extinctions through greenhouse warming, ocean acidification and sulfur-driven cooling pulses.

Exercise

  • (a)
    Pinatubo injected roughly 20 Tg of SO₂. If all of it is converted to H₂SO₄ (M = 98.08 g/mol, vs 64.07 for SO₂), what mass of H₂SO₄ results, and what mass of aerosol if the droplets are 75% H₂SO₄ by mass?
  • (b)
    If an aerosol loading of 30 Tg decays with an e-folding time of 1 year, what loading remains after 2 years?
  • (c)
    Estimate the peak radiative forcing for an AOD of 0.15, using −20 and −25 W m⁻² per unit AOD.
Show solution

Answers: (a) H₂SO₄ = 20 × 98.08/64.07 ≈ 30.6 Tg; aerosol = 30.6/0.75 ≈ 41 Tg. (b) 30 × e−2 = 30 × 0.135 ≈ 4.1 Tg. (c) −20 × 0.15 = −3.0 W m⁻² and −25 × 0.15 = −3.75 W m⁻², bracketing assessed values of about −3 W m⁻² — comparable in magnitude, for a year or two, to the total forcing from all anthropogenic CO₂ since pre-industrial times (~2 W m⁻²).
