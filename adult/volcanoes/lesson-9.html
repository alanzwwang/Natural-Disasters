Volcanic Ash and Aviation

Lesson 9 of 10

Overview12345678910Case studiesReferences

Key idea of this section

Volcanic ash melts in jet engines, abrades aircraft, and drifts invisibly for thousands of kilometres; a global network of nine advisory centres tracks it

Why ash is dangerous to aircraft

Volcanic ash consists of angular fragments of glass, crystals and rock, hard enough (Mohs hardness ~5–6) to abrade windscreens, leading edges and sensors. More critically, the glass in ash softens and melts at roughly 1,000–1,100 °C, below the operating temperature of modern jet-engine combustors (which commonly exceed ~1,400 °C). Molten ash deposits on turbine nozzle guide vanes, restricting airflow and causing engine surge and flameout. Ash also blocks pitot tubes (corrupting airspeed indications) and contaminates cabin air. Volcanic clouds are often hard to distinguish from meteorological clouds at night or in cloud, and onboard weather radar detects only large particles.

Notable encounters

On 24 June 1982, British Airways Flight 9, a Boeing 747, flew into ash from Galunggung (Java) at about 37,000 ft. All four engines flamed out; the aircraft glided to about 13,500 ft before the crew restarted the engines and landed in Jakarta with abraded windscreens. On 15 December 1989, KLM Flight 867, a new Boeing 747-400, lost all four engines in ash from Redoubt (Alaska), descended several thousand metres before restarting, and landed in Anchorage with about US$80 million in damage. Between the 1970s and 2010s, more than a hundred ash encounters were documented, several dozen causing significant damage. No fatal crash has been attributed to ash, but the risk of catastrophe led to the creation of an international warning system.

The International Airways Volcano Watch and VAACs

Under the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) International Airways Volcano Watch, nine Volcanic Ash Advisory Centres (VAACs) — Anchorage, Buenos Aires, Darwin, London, Montréal, Tokyo, Toulouse, Washington and Wellington — monitor their regions using satellite remote sensing (visible, infrared split-window brightness temperature differences, and SO₂ retrievals), pilot reports and observatory notifications, and run dispersion models (for example NAME at London VAAC, HYSPLIT at Washington and other centres) to issue Volcanic Ash Advisories and graphical forecasts of ash extent at multiple flight levels. Volcano observatories issue Volcano Observatory Notices for Aviation (VONAs) using the aviation colour code. The quality of an ash forecast depends crucially on the eruption source parameters — plume height, mass eruption rate, vertical mass distribution, and the fraction of fine ash that survives near-source fallout (often only a few per cent) — which are themselves uncertain by an order of magnitude.

Eyjafjallajökull 2010 and concentration thresholds

The April–May 2010 eruption of Eyjafjallajökull (Iceland), a modest VEI 4 event fragmented by interaction with glacial meltwater, produced fine ash carried by persistent northwesterly winds over northern Europe. Under the existing policy of zero tolerance of ash, much of European airspace closed between 15 and 20 April; about 100,000 flights were cancelled, around 10 million passengers were affected, and airlines lost an estimated US$1.7 billion (IATA). The crisis prompted the rapid introduction of quantitative ash-concentration limits. European regulators defined zones of low (< 2 × 10⁻³ g m⁻³), medium (2–4 × 10⁻³ g m⁻³) and high (> 4 × 10⁻³ g m⁻³) contamination, with operations allowed in some zones subject to airlines' safety cases. This shift from avoidance to risk management demanded quantitative forecasts of concentration, which remain far less certain than forecasts of ash presence, and fuelled research on ensemble modelling, data assimilation and engine tolerance testing.

Settling of fine ash

Small particles settle slowly and can remain aloft for days. For particles much smaller than ~100 μm, the Stokes law gives a terminal velocity:

vt = (ρp − ρa) g d² / (18 μ)

where μ is the dynamic viscosity of air (~1.5 × 10⁻⁵ Pa s in the upper troposphere, though temperature-dependent). Non-sphericity reduces settling velocities further, while aggregation increases them.

Exercise

  • (a)
    Compute the Stokes settling velocity of a 30 μm ash particle (density 2,500 kg m⁻³) in air with μ = 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ Pa s, and the time to fall 10 km. Repeat for 10 μm.
  • (b)
    A cloud contains 10⁹ kg of fine ash spread through a volume 500 km × 200 km × 2 km. What is the mean concentration, and how does it compare with the European thresholds?
Show solution

Answers: (a) vt = 2,500 × 9.81 × (30 × 10⁻⁶)² / (18 × 1.5 × 10⁻⁵) = 2.21 × 10⁻⁵ / 2.7 × 10⁻⁴ ≈ 0.082 m s⁻¹; time ≈ 10,000 / 0.082 ≈ 1.2 × 10⁵ s ≈ 34 hours. For 10 μm, vt is nine times smaller, ≈ 0.009 m s⁻¹, so the fall takes ~1.1 × 10⁶ s ≈ 13 days — such particles travel around the globe. (b) Volume = 5 × 10⁵ × 2 × 10⁵ × 2 × 10³ = 2 × 10¹⁴ m³; concentration = 10⁹ / 2 × 10¹⁴ = 5 × 10⁻⁶ kg m⁻³ = 5 mg m⁻³ = 5 × 10⁻³ g m⁻³ — above the 4 × 10⁻³ g m⁻³ high-contamination threshold, though real clouds are highly heterogeneous, with thin layers of much higher concentration.
