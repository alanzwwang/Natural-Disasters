Hazard Mapping and Risk Reduction

Lesson 10 of 10

Overview12345678910Case studiesReferences

Key idea of this section

Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability; volcanic risk is reduced most effectively by land-use planning, monitoring, warning and evacuation, informed by clear communication

From hazard to risk

Volcanic hazard is the probability that a given phenomenon of a given intensity affects a location in a given time window; exposure is what is in harm's way (people, buildings, infrastructure, livelihoods); and vulnerability is the probability of loss given exposure, depending on building type, the intensity of the phenomenon (e.g. dynamic pressure, temperature, ash load) and the preparedness of people. Risk combines all three, and is commonly expressed as annual expected loss or as individual risk (annual probability of death at a location). Worldwide, the Global Volcano Model consortium and GVP estimate that roughly 800 million people live within 100 km of a Holocene volcano, and that a small number of countries (Indonesia, the Philippines, Japan, Mexico, Ethiopia, Guatemala) account for most of the exposed population.

Hazard maps

Traditional hazard maps are based on the geological history of the volcano: mapping past deposits, dating them, and assuming that future eruptions will resemble past ones. Modern maps increasingly combine geology with numerical modelling and probability: tephra fall from thousands of simulations using historical wind fields (e.g. with Tephra2 or Fall3D), PDC inundation probabilities from energy-cone or depth-averaged models (e.g. Titan2D, IMEX_SfloW2D), and lahar inundation from empirical methods. A widely used semi-empirical tool is LAHARZ (Iverson, Schilling and Vallance, 1998), based on analysis of 27 lahar paths at 9 volcanoes, which relates lahar volume V to the inundated cross-sectional area A and planimetric area B:

A = 0.05 V2/3,     B = 200 V2/3

These relations are applied with digital elevation models for a range of hypothetical volumes, yielding nested inundation zones. Probabilistic maps must clearly express uncertainty, and hazard maps are only effective when they are understood and used — the Nevado del Ruiz map of October 1985 correctly showed Armero in the lahar path, but was not acted upon in time.

Risk-reduction strategies

Land-use planning is the most effective long-term measure, but is often politically difficult on fertile volcanic soils with high population density; Italy's evacuation plan for Vesuvius, covering ~600,000–700,000 people in the "red zone", is a prominent example of planning for low-probability, high-consequence scenarios. Engineering measures include lahar dikes and sabo dams (widely used in Japan and Indonesia), roofs designed for ash loads, lava-diversion barriers, and degassing of lakes. Monitoring and early warning, including automated lahar-detection systems (acoustic flow monitors and tripwires on Mount Rainier and elsewhere), allow evacuation. Effective communication requires trust between scientists, authorities and communities; the success at Pinatubo owed much to an effective videotape that showed officials what pyroclastic flows and lahars could do. The 1986 creation of the USGS–USAID Volcano Disaster Assistance Program (VDAP), a direct response to Armero, has since supported crisis responses at dozens of volcanoes worldwide.

Statistics of fatalities

Auker et al. (2013) found that pyroclastic density currents are responsible for roughly one-third of recorded volcanic fatalities since 1600, with lahars, tsunamis, and indirect effects (famine and disease) also major contributors, and that most PDC fatalities occur within about 10 km of the vent, though lahars and tsunamis extend lethal reach to many tens of kilometres. The number of fatalities per event has fallen over time in regions with monitoring, but growing populations around volcanoes keep exposure increasing.

Open questions

Current research targets include the physics of magma mush remobilisation and the timescales over which eruptible magma accumulates (from diffusion chronometry of crystals, suggesting days to centuries); improved eruption forecasting using machine learning on multiparameter data; the long-term hazard of super-eruptions; better ensemble forecasting of ash dispersion; and the cascading impacts of eruptions on globalised systems (supply chains, telecommunications cables, aviation, food security) — the "volcanic black swan" scenario of a future VEI 7 eruption, for which humanity is poorly prepared.

Exercise

  • (a)
    Using LAHARZ, estimate the inundated cross-sectional area A and planimetric area B for a lahar of 10⁷ m³. How much does B increase for a lahar of 10⁸ m³?
  • (b)
    A lahar travels at a mean speed of 10 m s⁻¹ towards a town 60 km downstream. How long is the potential warning time after detection at the source?
  • (c)
    A town has an annual probability of lahar inundation of 1% and expected losses of $500 million if inundated. What is the annual expected loss?
Show solution

Answers: (a) V2/3 = (10⁷)2/3 ≈ 46,400; A = 0.05 × 46,400 ≈ 2,300 m²; B = 200 × 46,400 ≈ 9.3 × 10⁶ m² ≈ 9.3 km². For 10⁸ m³, V2/3 ≈ 215,400, so B ≈ 43 km² — 4.6 times larger (102/3 ≈ 4.64) for ten times the volume. (b) t = 60,000 / 10 = 6,000 s ≈ 1.7 hours — similar to the ~2.5 hours that passed between the Ruiz eruption and the lahar's arrival at Armero, enough time to evacuate if warnings are issued and acted upon. (c) 0.01 × $500 million = $5 million per year — the benchmark against which the annualised cost of mitigation (such as dikes or relocation) can be judged.
