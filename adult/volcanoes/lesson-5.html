Eruption Columns and Plume Dynamics

Lesson 5 of 10

Overview12345678910Case studiesReferences

Key idea of this section

A Plinian column rises because it entrains and heats air until it becomes less dense than the atmosphere; if it cannot entrain enough air quickly enough, it collapses into pyroclastic density currents

Structure of an eruption column

A sustained eruption column has three regions. The gas-thrust region, a few hundred metres to a few kilometres high, is a momentum-driven jet: the erupting mixture leaves the vent at speeds of order 100–600 m s⁻¹, but because it is largely solid and liquid particles by mass, it is initially several times denser than the atmosphere. The jet entrains ambient air through turbulent eddies at its margins; the air is heated by the hot pyroclasts and expands, so the mixture density drops. If the density falls below that of the atmosphere before the jet's momentum is exhausted, the column enters the convective region and rises buoyantly as a plume. It eventually reaches a level of neutral buoyancy in the stably stratified atmosphere, overshoots by momentum, and spreads laterally as an umbrella cloud — a gravity current that can extend hundreds of kilometres even upwind.

Buoyant plume theory

Morton, Taylor and Turner (1956) modelled turbulent plumes by assuming that the inflow velocity of entrained air is proportional to the plume's local vertical velocity, with an entrainment coefficient of about 0.1. For a plume from a maintained point source of buoyancy flux F₀ (units m⁴ s⁻³) rising through an atmosphere of constant buoyancy frequency N, dimensional analysis and the MTT equations give:

H = C F₀1/4 N−3/4

with C of order 5–8 depending on the entrainment coefficient and whether the maximum or neutral-buoyancy height is meant. Because the buoyancy flux is proportional to the thermal energy flux, and thus to the mass eruption rate, column height scales with the fourth root of eruption rate: a tenfold increase in eruption rate raises the column by only a factor of ~1.8. For practical use, Mastin et al. (2009) fitted historical eruptions to the empirical relation:

H = 2.00 V̇0.241

where H is the plume height above the vent in km and V̇ the DRE volumetric eruption rate in m³ s⁻¹. The scatter is large — roughly a factor of four in eruption rate for a given height — and wind strongly bends weak plumes, reducing their rise height; models such as those of Woods (1988) and Bursik (2001) include moisture, wind and particle fallout. Latent heat released by condensation of entrained water vapour can add several kilometres to the height of a column in a humid tropical atmosphere.

Column collapse

If the mixture at the top of the gas-thrust region remains denser than the atmosphere, the column collapses and the mass falls back as a fountain feeding pyroclastic density currents. Wilson, Sparks and Walker (1980) showed that collapse is favoured by low exit velocity (low gas content), large vent radius (less air entrained relative to mass flux, since entrainment scales with surface area while mass flux scales with cross-sectional area), and low magma temperature. As a Plinian eruption progresses, vent erosion widens the conduit and the eruption may evolve from a stable column to partial and then total collapse — the classical sequence of Plinian fall overlain by ignimbrite seen at Vesuvius in AD 79 and in many caldera-forming eruptions. Partial collapse, with simultaneous buoyant plume and currents, is common and is now explored with three-dimensional multiphase numerical models.

Overpressured vents and supersonic jets

Eruption velocities are limited by the speed of sound in the gas–particle mixture, which can be only tens to ~100 m s⁻¹ because the particles add mass but not stiffness. At the vent, flow is often choked; if exit pressure exceeds atmospheric, the jet expands supersonically, forming shock structures (Mach discs) that have been imaged in laboratory and numerical experiments and inferred from infrasound. The 2022 Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai plume reached about 57 km, into the mesosphere, the highest volcanic plume ever measured, illustrating the extreme energetics possible when vast amounts of magma and seawater interact.

Tephra sedimentation

Particles fall out of the column margins and from the umbrella cloud. Coarse lapilli fall close to the vent; fine ash is carried hundreds to thousands of kilometres. Very fine ash (< 30–60 μm) commonly falls as aggregates, which brings it down faster and nearer to the vent than its individual settling velocity implies, producing secondary thickness maxima such as the one observed ~300 km downwind of Mount St. Helens in 1980.
