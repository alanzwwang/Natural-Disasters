Magma Rheology and Petrology

Lesson 2 of 10

Overview12345678910Case studiesReferences

Key idea of this section

Viscosity spans more than eleven orders of magnitude, from ~10 Pa s for hot basalt to >10¹² Pa s for degassed rhyolite, and it controls whether gas escapes quietly or explosively

Composition and classification

Volcanic rocks are classified chemically by silica and total alkalis (the TAS diagram). In approximate terms, basalt contains 45–52 wt% SiO₂, basaltic andesite 52–57%, andesite 57–63%, dacite 63–~69%, and rhyolite more than ~69%. Eruption temperatures fall as silica rises: basalts erupt at roughly 1,100–1,250 °C, andesites at 950–1,100 °C, dacites at 800–1,000 °C and rhyolites at 700–900 °C. Silica-rich melts are also typically richer in dissolved water, because water is concentrated in the residual liquid as anhydrous crystals form.

Melt structure and viscosity

Silicate melts are networks of SiO₄ tetrahedra linked by bridging oxygens. Network-forming cations (Si⁴⁺, Al³⁺ when charge-balanced) increase polymerisation and viscosity; network modifiers (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺, Na⁺, K⁺) and dissolved water break bridging bonds and lower viscosity. Over the range of eruption temperatures, melt viscosity follows a non-Arrhenian Vogel–Fulcher–Tammann (VFT) form:

log₁₀ η = A + B / (T − C)

where T is in kelvin and A, B, C depend on composition. The model of Giordano, Russell and Dingwell (2008) fits a common A ≈ −4.55 and composition-dependent B and C, and is widely used. Typical melt viscosities are:

Composition       T (°C)        η (Pa s)
Basalt            1,150–1,250   10¹–10³
Andesite          1,000–1,100   10³–10⁶
Dacite            900–1,000     10⁵–10⁸
Hydrous rhyolite  750–850       10⁶–10⁹
Degassed rhyolite 750–850       10¹⁰–10¹²

Adding 1 wt% of water to a dry rhyolite can lower its viscosity by two to three orders of magnitude; conversely, degassing during ascent raises viscosity enormously, which is one reason why ascending silicic magma can "freeze" in the conduit and why lava domes are so stiff.

Crystals and bubbles

Natural magmas are suspensions. Crystals raise the effective viscosity, modestly at low concentrations and catastrophically as the crystal fraction φ approaches the maximum packing fraction φm (typically ~0.5–0.7, depending on crystal shape and size distribution). A simple relation is the Einstein–Roscoe equation:

ηeff = ηmelt (1 − φ/φm)−2.5

Above roughly 40–50% crystals a magma develops a yield strength and behaves as a Bingham or Herschel–Bulkley fluid; above ~60% it behaves essentially as a solid "mush" that cannot erupt without remobilisation (for example by reheating or gas sparging from fresh basalt injected from below). Bubbles can either increase or decrease viscosity depending on the capillary number, i.e. on whether they deform in the flow.

Lava flow dynamics

A lava flow on a slope is driven by gravity and resisted by viscous stresses. For a wide sheet of Newtonian fluid of thickness h, density ρ and viscosity η on a slope θ, the Jeffreys equation gives the mean velocity:

ū = ρ g h² sin θ / (3 η)

Basaltic flows advance at metres per second near the vent (and up to tens of km/h in confined channels), whereas andesite and dacite lavas move metres per day and dacitic domes grow by extrusion of near-solid magma. As lava cools, a crust develops and the flow becomes rheologically complex: basaltic flows form smooth, ropy pāhoehoe at low strain rates and clinkery ʻaʻā at higher strain rates and viscosities. Flow length is limited by effusion rate and cooling, which is why high effusion rates produce long flows (Walker's classical observation).

Exercise

  • (a)
    A basaltic melt with 30% crystals has a melt viscosity of 100 Pa s. Taking φm = 0.6, what is the effective viscosity? Repeat for 45% crystals.
  • (b)
    Estimate the mean velocity of a 2-m-thick basaltic lava sheet (ρ = 2,600 kg m⁻³, η = 1,000 Pa s) on a 5° slope.
Show solution

Answers: (a) For φ = 0.30: (1 − 0.5)−2.5 = 0.5−2.5 ≈ 5.66, so ηeff ≈ 570 Pa s. For φ = 0.45: (1 − 0.75)−2.5 = 0.25−2.5 = 32, so ηeff ≈ 3,200 Pa s — a sixfold increase for only 15 extra percentage points of crystals. (b) ū = 2,600 × 9.81 × 2² × sin 5° / (3 × 1,000) = 2,600 × 9.81 × 4 × 0.0872 / 3,000 ≈ 3.0 m s⁻¹, about 11 km/h — consistent with observed velocities of channelised Hawaiian flows near the vent.
