Radar Observation of Tornadoes

Lesson 8 of 10

Key idea of this section

vN = λ·PRF / 4,   rmax = c / (2·PRF)   →   vN rmax = cλ / 8 (the Doppler dilemma)

Doppler radar principles

A Doppler weather radar measures the phase shift between successive pulses scattered by hydrometeors and debris, giving the radial (along-beam) component of target velocity. The maximum unambiguous velocity (Nyquist velocity) and the maximum unambiguous range trade off against each other through the pulse repetition frequency: for the US WSR-88D (an S-band radar, λ ≈ 10–11 cm), a PRF of 1,000 Hz gives vN ≈ 27 m/s and rmax = 150 km. Velocities larger than vN are aliased (folded) and must be unfolded by algorithms or by eye. The WSR-88D network (about 160 radars, deployed 1990–1997 and upgraded to dual polarization in 2011–2013) has a half-power beamwidth of about 0.95°.

Beam geometry

Two geometric facts limit tornado detection. First, the beam rises above the ground with range because of its elevation angle and Earth's curvature; with the standard 4/3-Earth-radius refraction model:

h = √(r² + R'² + 2 r R' sin θ) − R',   R' = (4/3) × 6,371 km ≈ 8,495 km

At 100 km range the centre of the 0.5° beam is about 1.5 km above the radar level, so shallow circulations and the near-ground tornado itself are not sampled. Second, the beam broadens linearly with range (≈ r × beamwidth in radians), reaching about 1.7 km at 100 km — far wider than most tornadoes. Velocity extremes are therefore smoothed, and a tornado appears as a strong "gate-to-gate" shear signature rather than a resolved vortex. Mobile radars (e.g. Doppler on Wheels, RaXPol) deployed a few kilometres from tornadoes, with beamwidths of 0.9–1.0°, achieve resolution of tens of metres, which is how the highest tornado wind speeds have been measured.

Reflectivity signatures

Classic supercells display a hook or appendage echo on the rear flank, wrapping around the mesocyclone, a weak-echo region or BWER beneath the overhanging precipitation aloft where the updraft is strongest, and, in strong tornadoes, a "debris ball" — a small, roughly circular area of high reflectivity at the hook tip produced by lofted debris. In very intense tornadoes, a radial "debris spike" of spurious echo can appear down-range of the debris ball, caused by multiple scattering (three-body-type and multipath effects) from the dense debris cloud, as in the Mayfield 2021 tornado.

Velocity signatures

In storm-relative velocity, a mesocyclone appears as an azimuthally adjacent pair of inbound (negative, towards the radar) and outbound (positive) velocities — a couplet — with cyclonic sense (outbound to the right when looking from the radar). The rotational velocity is commonly defined as

Vrot = (Vmax,out − Vmax,in) / 2,   with inbound velocities negative

and azimuthal shear as ΔV / D, where D is the distance between the extremes. A tornadic vortex signature (TVS) is an intense, gate-to-gate (adjacent azimuths) couplet, typically extending through several elevation angles; the original TVS was identified by NSSL in the 1973 Union City, Oklahoma, tornado. Studies of thousands of WSR-88D tornado cases (e.g. Smith et al., 2015) show that the probability of a given EF rating increases strongly with low-level Vrot, conditioned on range and the near-storm environment; such relationships underpin modern warning decision guidance.

Dual-polarization signatures

Dual-polarization radars transmit and receive both horizontally and vertically polarised waves, yielding differential reflectivity (ZDR), the co-polar correlation coefficient (ρhv) and specific differential phase (KDP). Hydrometeors have ρhv close to 1 (above ~0.97 for rain); debris — irregular, randomly oriented and of mixed composition — has much lower values. The tornadic debris signature (TDS) (Ryzhkov et al., 2005) is a localised region of high reflectivity (typically > 40–45 dBZ), low ρhv (often < 0.8–0.9) and near-zero or negative ZDR, collocated with a strong velocity couplet. A TDS confirms that a tornado is in progress and lofting debris, often at night or in rain-wrapped situations where spotters cannot see it. The height to which debris is lofted correlates broadly with tornado intensity. Other dual-pol features in supercells include the ZDR arc along the forward-flank reflectivity gradient (indicating size sorting by strong storm-relative helicity) and the ZDR column (a column of large raindrops lofted above the melting level, marking the updraft).

Emerging technologies

Rapid-update scanning (SAILS and MESO-SAILS, which insert extra low-level scans), phased-array radars that scan volumes in about one minute, gap-filling X-band networks (e.g. CASA), and rapid-scan mobile radars are improving temporal resolution, which matters because tornadogenesis can occur within the 4–5 minutes of a conventional volume scan. The US is planning a successor to the WSR-88D network, likely based on phased-array technology.

Exercise

  • (a)
    An S-band radar has λ = 10.7 cm. Compute vN and rmax for PRF = 1,000 Hz and the product vN rmax.
  • (b)
    Compute the height of the 0.5° beam centre and the beam width at 100 km range (beamwidth 0.95°).
  • (c)
    A couplet has a maximum outbound velocity of +45 m/s and a maximum inbound of −40 m/s, 1 km apart. Compute Vrot and the azimuthal shear.
Show solution

Answers: (a) vN = 0.107 × 1,000 / 4 ≈ 26.8 m/s; rmax = 3 × 108 / 2,000 = 150 km; vN rmax = cλ/8 ≈ 4.0 × 106 m²/s. Doubling PRF doubles vN but halves rmax. (b) h = √(r² + R'² + 2rR' sin 0.5°) − R' ≈ 1.46 km; beam width ≈ 100 km × 0.95° × π/180 ≈ 1.66 km. (c) Vrot = (45 − (−40)) / 2 = 42.5 m/s; azimuthal shear = 85 / 1,000 = 0.085 s−1 — an intense, likely tornadic circulation at that range.
